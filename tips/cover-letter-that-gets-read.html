How to Write a Cover Letter That Actually Gets Read

A simple structure for short, specific cover letters, with examples for freshers and for experienced professionals.

Most cover letters are never read, and you should know that before you spend an evening on one. The ones that get opened get opened for a reason: a human is unsure about your resume, curious about a switch, or choosing between two similar people, and the letter is where they look for an answer. This article shows you when a letter is worth writing, how to keep it short enough to be read, and exactly what goes in each paragraph.

What a cover letter can and cannot do

Think of the cover letter as a tiebreaker, not a ticket in. Screening starts with the resume, and the letter usually gets opened when the resume raises a question it cannot answer itself. A letter will not rescue a mismatched application, and no reader will forgive thin experience because your prose is warm. What it can do is settle a doubt, explain a jump, and make a borderline resume feel intentional.

Write one in these situations:

  • The posting asks for one. If it says attach or paste a cover letter, that is part of the application. Treat it as required, whatever you think of letters.
  • You are changing fields. A salesperson moving into customer success, a teacher moving into corporate training: the resume shows the past, the letter connects it to the future.
  • Something needs one sentence of explanation. A career gap, a short stint, a relocation, a return after a break. Explaining it calmly on your own terms beats letting the reader guess.
  • The employer is small. At a ten-person startup, a single school, or a family-run firm, the owner or the manager reads the letters personally, because they are choosing a colleague, not filling a slot.
  • Communication is part of the job. For sales, teaching, support, account management, or any writing-adjacent role, the letter is a work sample.
  • Someone referred you. Naming the person who pointed you to the role, with their permission, is the strongest opening line available.

When the opposite is true, put your hours elsewhere. A large company with a long portal form, a role where your resume matches requirement for requirement, and high-volume hiring that screens mostly on structured fields: in those cases the letter is optional and often never displayed. Spend the time on your resume and on the form questions instead. Note that many Indian job portals and some American ones offer only a small text box instead of an upload. That box is not a place for a full letter. Two tight sentences, one about fit and one about availability, are enough.

How long it should be

Three to four short paragraphs, roughly 250 to 400 words. Attached as a document, it fits on one page. Pasted into an email, it fits on one screen. Length is not where effort shows; discipline is.

A quick test: read the letter aloud once. If it takes more than a couple of minutes, or if you hear yourself reciting your resume, cut it. The reader will give your letter a fraction of the time you give it, so front-load the strongest material and let nothing slow the read. Every sentence should do one of three jobs: connect you to this job, prove a claim, or make the next step easy.

A structure that works, paragraph by paragraph

Almost every letter that earns a reply follows the same four-part shape: an opening tied to this company and role, two short paragraphs of proof, and a clear close. Learn the shape once and adapt it for years.

Paragraph 1: the opening

Name the role, say where you found it, and give one specific reason it fits you. Compare the two most common openings in the world with one that does actual work:

Weak: "To Whom It May Concern, I am writing to apply for the position advertised on your website. I believe I am a good fit for this role."

Strong: "Dear Ms. Rao, I am applying for the Junior Accountant role posted on the Kalyani Foods careers page. I finished my B.Com this spring and spent six months handling GST filings at a CA firm, so the daily work described in your advertisement is work I have already done."

Use a name if you can find one; the posting, the company site, or a professional networking profile usually reveals it. When you cannot, "Dear Hiring Team" beats "Dear Sir/Madam", which beats "To Whom It May Concern". Verify every name and title before sending, because a wrong name undoes the whole letter.

Paragraphs 2 and 3: the proof

Pick the two claims from your resume that matter most to this employer and expand each with one concrete detail. The resume documents; the letter argues. Do not walk through your history in order. Choose the internship closest to this job's duties, the project that proves you can do the hard part, the result your current manager would vouch for.

Concrete beats impressive. "I led a team" says less than "I trained two new hires on our ticketing system and covered the shift roster through the festival season". Numbers are welcome when they are yours: clients handled, students taught, deliveries routed, guests served, tickets closed. One or two specifics per paragraph is plenty; this is a letter, not an inventory.

Paragraph 4: the close

Close with a clear, calm ask and any practical detail the reader needs. Say you would welcome an interview, mention your availability or notice period if relevant, and stop. No pleading, and no "I hope to hear from you at your earliest convenience".

"I would welcome the chance to discuss how my internship work maps to this role. I can join from the first week of June, and I am happy to take a practical test at interview. Thank you for your time."

A full example for a fresher

Freshers often assume they have nothing to argue with. You have more than you think: internships, class projects, the family shop's books, the college fest you handled money for, the hostel committee you ran. The trick is to connect one of those to the job's actual duties and let it carry the letter. Here is a complete example, for a fresher accountant applying to a mid-sized company.

Dear Ms. Rao,

I am applying for the Junior Accountant role at Kalyani Foods, which I found on your careers page. I completed my B.Com in May with accounting and taxation as my specialization, and I spent the last six months as an accounts intern at a CA firm in Pune, so the work your advertisement describes, from GST returns to vendor reconciliations, is work I have already done under supervision.

At the firm I prepared monthly GST returns for fourteen small clients and built a simple Excel tracker that flagged pending bills before their due dates, which cut the hours spent chasing invoices. I work comfortably in Tally and in Excel, including VLOOKUP and pivot tables, and I keep a practice set of books for a family business so the routines stay sharp between jobs.

I want to join Kalyani Foods specifically because your accounts team handles costing for your own plants, which means a junior accountant here sees the full cycle from purchase order to payment rather than one slice of it. That is the training I want from my first role.

I can join from the first week of June and would welcome the chance to discuss my application. Thank you for your time.

Priya Deshmukh

Notice what the letter never does. It does not apologize for being a fresher, does not list software it has only watched tutorials on, and does not say "hardworking and dynamic". Every claim comes attached to a specific thing the writer did, and the reader can check any of them in an interview.

A full example for an experienced professional

With experience, the letter's job changes: you are no longer proving you can do the work, you are proving you want this work, here. The strongest experienced letters take two highlights and go deep, then answer the question the resume cannot: why this move. Here is an example for a nurse applying to a new hospital.

Dear Hiring Committee,

I am applying for the Medical-Surgical Staff Nurse position at Lakeview Hospital, which I saw on your careers page. For the past five years I have worked med-surg at a 300-bed community hospital, including two years as a charge nurse, and your new oncology unit is exactly the setting I have been building toward.

On my current unit I precept new nurses, most recently a group of four graduate nurses through their first solo shifts, and I have kept my chemotherapy administration certification current for three years. After our unit went a full year without a fall-related injury, my manager asked me to lead our falls-prevention huddle, and the checklist I built for it is now used on two other floors.

I am drawn to Lakeview because your oncology unit pairs a strong nurse-to-patient ratio with a patient education program, and I want to work where teaching patients is part of the job rather than an add-on. I hold an active state license and can start within a month of an offer.

Thank you for considering my application. I would welcome an interview.

Alana Reyes, RN

The skeleton transfers to any field. A teacher shows one classroom result and why this school. A salesperson shows a territory turnaround and why this market. A hotelier shows the season they kept guest scores up and why this property. Swap the nouns, keep the shape.

One base letter, many applications, no template smell

You will send many letters, and writing each from scratch is a recipe for either exhaustion or genericness. The workable middle is a base letter with slots: the frame stays yours, the filled parts change every time.

  • Slot 1: the company, the role, and where you found it.
  • Slot 2: the one reason this employer or role fits you, drawn from the posting itself.
  • Slot 3: the proof paragraph closest to this job's duties.
  • Slot 4: the practical close, meaning your notice period, start date, or license.

Spend your tailoring minutes on slots 2 and 3, because they are the parts a reader can smell. Read the finished letter once, aloud, and ask one question: could I send this, unchanged, to the company across the street? If yes, it is still too generic. Add something only this company would make true.

When the cover letter is the email itself

Plenty of applications still arrive by email, especially at smaller firms in India and through referrals anywhere. When the email is the letter, follow the posting's instructions first: if it names a subject line format, use it exactly, because employers filter incoming mail that way. If it does not, use "Application for [role], [location], [your name]".

Keep the body short: greeting, role, one or two lines of proof, closing line, attachment note. Save the full letter for a PDF attachment when the posting asks for one.

Subject: Application for Sales Executive, Mumbai, Rahul Mehta

Dear Ms. Kulkarni,

I am applying for the Sales Executive role in Mumbai that your team posted last week. I have sold FMCG products to kirana stores for four years at Venkat Distributors, where I grew my territory from 90 to 140 active outlets and now run two of the company's largest beats.

My resume is attached. I would welcome a conversation about the role and can come in any day this week.

Thank you for your time,

Rahul Mehta

The small things carry weight here. Attach the resume as a PDF named with your name and the role, not "resume final 2". Send it to the exact address given, and put your phone number under your name. One polite follow-up after a week or so is reasonable; daily reminders are not.

The mistakes that get letters deleted

Most letters die from a handful of habits. Check yours against this list before it goes out.

  • Restating the resume. The reader has the resume open. If the letter repeats it, it adds nothing. Argue, do not inventory.
  • The find-and-replace letter. Swapping only the company name is easy to spot, and getting the name wrong is fatal. Personalize the opening and the proof, or do not send it.
  • Hollow adjectives. "Dynamic, results-driven professional" is unreadable. Replace each adjective with something you did.
  • All about you. "This role will help me grow my career" is half an argument. Lead with what the employer gets, then what you want.
  • Two pages. Nobody reads page two. If it does not fit on one page, it is not a letter yet, it is a draft.
  • Salary talk. Compensation belongs in form fields and negotiation, not in the letter. There is a separate article on how to answer common job application questions.
  • Confessions. Do not open with your weaknesses, your reason for leaving, or a layoff story. Answer those where they are asked, briefly and factually.
  • Typos in the first two lines. The opening is the only part guaranteed to be read. Proofread it twice.

Final checklist before you send

  • The posting asks for a letter, or your resume raises a question the letter answers.
  • Three to four paragraphs, 250 to 400 words, one page maximum.
  • The opening names the role, the source, and one specific reason this job fits you.
  • Two proof paragraphs, each with one concrete, checkable detail.
  • A close with a clear ask and the practical detail: notice period, start date, license.
  • Company name, role title, manager's name, and attachment filename all verified.
  • Sent within days of the posting going up, not weeks.

The letter is one part of a working application, and it cannot fix a weak resume, so make sure yours holds up first: see how to write a resume that gets interviews. And remember that a letter only matters if the right people eventually see it, which is a volume question; that side of the search is covered in how many jobs you should apply to.
