How to Prepare for Campus Placements

Aptitude tests, group discussions, technical rounds and HR interviews: a preparation plan for campus hiring.

Campus placement season arrives fast: one week you are attending lectures, the next there is a shortlist on the placement notice board and an online test on Sunday morning. The process rewards preparation more than talent, and the preparation is knowable. This guide explains how campus hiring in India actually runs, what each round tests, and how to practise for each one, whether you are in engineering, commerce, nursing, design or anything else.

How campus hiring actually works

Your college's placement cell sits at the centre of everything. It invites companies, publishes their criteria, collects applications, allots slots and enforces the rules. Companies that come to campus are usually hiring several people at once, so they filter students in stages rather than reading every resume line by line.

Eligibility and cutoffs

Before every drive, the placement cell publishes the company's criteria. The most common is a minimum CGPA or percentage: a company might set its cutoff at 7.0, which means a student at 6.9 cannot sit for that drive no matter how good the interview would have been. Other common filters are no active backlogs, minimum marks in 10th and 12th, limits on gap years, and sometimes restrictions on which branches or courses can apply.

Check the criteria before you get emotionally invested in any company. If your CGPA sits below most cutoffs, ask your placement cell early in the semester which companies are realistic for you, and put your energy into drives you can actually enter, plus an off campus search where cutoffs often matter less.

Mass recruiters and dream companies

Mass recruiters hire in large numbers across many colleges: large IT services firms, banks, insurance companies, retail chains, logistics companies and hospital groups that take whole batches of nurses. Their process is standardised. They lean heavily on aptitude tests and short interviews, and they use academic cutoffs mainly to manage the sheer volume of applicants.

Dream companies, usually product firms, startups, design studios and niche consultancies, hire far fewer people and interview much more deeply. They often care less about your CGPA and more about what you can actually do: write code, design interfaces, model a machine, close a sale or care for patients. Many of them skip campus drives entirely and hire through internships and off campus openings instead.

Prepare differently for each. For mass recruiters, speed and accuracy on tests matter most, because the test is the biggest filter. For dream companies, depth matters: fewer topics, known thoroughly, with projects you can defend for half an hour.

And do not treat a mass recruiter offer as a consolation prize. It is a salary, a brand on your resume and a platform you can leverage into a better role a year or two later.

The one offer rule

Most colleges follow some version of "one student, one offer": the moment you accept an offer, you are out of further drives. Some colleges allow an upgrade, meaning you may keep attending companies that pay more, while others cap the number of drives you can sit for in total. Policies differ widely, so get yours in writing from the placement cell, including what counts as "accepting" and whether a dream offer unlocks an upgrade window.

This rule should change your strategy. If your college allows only one offer, never sit for a drive casually: an offer you accept in week two of the season ends your season. If upgrades are allowed, attending every drive you are eligible for becomes close to risk free, so do exactly that.

The rounds, in order

Not every company runs every round, but the sequence rarely changes:

  • Pre-placement talk: the company presents itself and describes the role. Attendance is often compulsory, and the talk tells you what the job actually involves, which is useful ammunition for later rounds.
  • Aptitude or online test: the first real filter, often eliminating more candidates than the interviews do.
  • Group discussion or group exercise: a check of how you argue, listen and behave in a group. Some companies use a case discussion, a role play or a team task instead.
  • Technical interview: your core subjects, projects and problem solving. There may be one or two of these rounds.
  • HR interview: communication, fit, relocation, salary expectations and honesty.
  • Offer: announced through the placement cell, usually days or weeks later.

The format shifts with the field. Nursing drives often include a practical demonstration or a viva on procedures. Sales roles frequently add a live role play where you pitch a product to the interviewer. Design roles replace the technical round with a portfolio review. Accounting drives may ask you to pass journal entries or work through a small case. Ask seniors which format each visiting company used last year, because placement processes are remarkably consistent from one year to the next.

Your preparation timeline

If you have two months

Week one: build a one page resume, list the core subjects of your branch, and take one untimed aptitude test to find your weak areas. Weeks two to six: daily aptitude practice in short timed sets, revision of one core subject at a time, and a written two minute explanation of every project on your resume. From week five, add one mock interview a week with a placed senior.

Weeks seven and eight: switch from learning to performing. Take full length tests under real timing, run group discussion practice with friends twice a week, and research the companies whose visits are announced: the role they are hiring for, their test pattern from previous years, and their interview style.

If you have two weeks

You cannot cover everything, so triage. Fix the resume first, because it enters every room before you do. Rehearse answers to the ten most common HR questions. Revise the three core subjects most likely to be asked in your branch, and do one timed aptitude set daily. One honest mock interview with a senior is worth more than three more days of silent reading.

The night before a drive

Revise nothing new. Lay out your clothes, print two copies of your resume, keep your ID and academic documents ready, and confirm the reporting time. Then sleep properly: a tired brain fails aptitude tests that a rested brain passes.

The aptitude or online test

What gets asked

Most tests mix three sections. Quantitative aptitude covers percentages, ratios, profit and loss, time and work, speed and distance, averages, permutations and probability. Logical reasoning covers seating arrangements, syllogisms, blood relations, series and data interpretation. Verbal ability covers reading comprehension, sentence correction and fill in the blanks.

Many companies add a fourth section tied to the role. Software roles include coding questions or a short programming test. Accounting roles test basic bookkeeping and sometimes GST. Nursing tests can include dosage calculations and fundamentals of patient care. Logistics roles lean on quantitative and data interpretation questions. Get each company's actual pattern from seniors rather than assuming they are all identical.

How to practise

  • Practise in short timed sets: twenty questions in twenty five minutes teaches more than two unhurried hours, because the clock is half the exam.
  • Keep an error log. After each set, note why every wrong answer happened: a concept gap, a careless slip or slow speed. Concept gaps need theory, speed needs repetition, carelessness needs a habit of rechecking.
  • Work on a computer with rough paper beside you, because that is the real setup.
  • Take one full length mock test every week in the final month, and review it afterwards line by line.

Worked examples show you what "know it cold" means. A bag is marked at 1,200 rupees with a 20 percent discount, so the selling price is 960; if the shopkeeper's cost was 800, the profit is 160, which is 20 percent of cost. You should reach that answer in well under a minute, without a calculator, because that is the pace the test demands. The same standard applies to reasoning: in a seating arrangement puzzle, your job is to draw the arrangement quickly and test each statement against it, not to solve it in your head to impress anyone.

On test day

Read every instruction: some tests have negative marking, some do not, and section order and time limits differ. Attempt your strongest section first to bank marks and confidence. If a question resists after about a minute, mark it and move on; an unanswered question costs nothing, but five minutes spent wrestling one question costs you five other questions.

The group discussion

What the panel scores

A GD panel scores content, communication, listening and behaviour. You do not need to speak the most; you need to make a few clear points, respond to what others actually said, and help the group move forward. The students who get cut are usually the silent ones and the shouters. The ones who get selected are the ones who sound easy to work with.

A sample opening

Entering early helps, but only with structure, not noise. When the topic is announced and you get a moment to think, take the frame:

Before we pick sides, it might help to split this topic into parts. It affects individuals, businesses and the government differently, so I'd suggest we take each part for a few minutes and then try to conclude. I'll start with individuals: for most people the core question is attention, how much of it social media takes and what it gives back in return.

A strong closing entry works too. Near the end, briefly summarise the group's main points and state the conclusion the discussion supports. It proves you were listening, and panels reward that heavily.

Topics and mistakes

Practise with real topics from the news, such as whether remote work is here to stay or whether advertising does more good than harm, and with abstract one word topics, which some panels love. Rotate a friend into the role of the disruptive candidate so you learn to stay calm when someone talks over you. Do not battle the disruptor directly; address the group instead, with something like "let me add to the point she raised earlier, because it answers what we were just debating."

The most common mistakes are all avoidable: repeating your own point louder instead of building on someone else's, attacking a person rather than a point, speaking once at the start and then vanishing, and looking at the panel instead of the group.

The technical round

Core subjects beat everything

Interviewers rarely wander far from fundamentals. Pick three or four core subjects for your field and learn them properly rather than skimming ten. For computer related branches that is usually data structures, databases, operating systems and networks. For mechanical it might be thermodynamics, strength of materials and manufacturing processes. For electronics, digital electronics, signals and communication systems. Nursing students should own anatomy, pharmacology basics and common ward procedures. Commerce students should be able to walk from a journal entry to a trial balance without notes.

Example questions to prepare for

  • Software: find a bug in this snippet, explain what an index does in a database, or walk through how you would design a simple feature.
  • Accounting: pass the journal entries for a credit sale, then explain how it lands in the balance sheet.
  • Nursing: describe how you would calculate an IV drip rate, and what you would check before administering a new drug.
  • Logistics: explain how you would decide how much stock a warehouse should hold.
  • Sales: pitch this pen to me, or tell me how you would plan a day of client visits.

Notice the pattern: these are work sized questions, not textbook recitals. Practise answering them aloud, in three or four sentences each, the way you would explain to a smart friend.

Know your own projects cold

Every line on your resume is an interview question in disguise. For each project and internship, prepare a two minute story: the problem, what you personally did, the tools you used, the result, and what you would improve if you redid it. If you cannot confidently explain a line on your resume, delete the line before the drive.

When you do not know

Say so, then reason aloud. "I haven't studied this topic, but based on what I know about X, my guess is Y, because Z" is a strong answer: it shows honesty and thinking. Bluffing is the fastest way to fail a technical round, because the interviewer simply keeps following the bluff until it collapses.

The resume for campus

One page. Campus interviewers read dozens of resumes per drive, often for a few minutes each, and a one page resume respects that. Structure it in this order: education, projects and internships, skills, certifications and achievements. Write achievement lines with a result where you can. "Handled accounts for our college fest" is weaker than "reconciled sponsor payments for our college fest and closed the books a week early."

Because the placement cell often submits a single resume on your behalf to many companies, keep a master version, and customise a copy whenever you know which company and role is coming. Cut adjectives like hardworking and passionate; they carry no information. And never list a skill you would fail an interview question on, because that is exactly where the technical round will probe.

Mock interviews with seniors

Seniors who went through last year's drives know things no general book can teach you: which company's test had a coding section, which panel liked puzzles, what the GD topics were, and how the HR round drifted from the script. This knowledge sits one year above you, and most of it goes unused because nobody asks.

Ask your placement cell, department seniors or alumni group for one mock interview each with two or three seniors who got placed in companies you are targeting. Give them your resume and the company name, and ask them to run it like the real thing. Then ask for brutal feedback, not encouragement. Record the session on your phone if they allow it; almost everyone is surprised by their own fillers and pace when they hear themselves.

Between senior mocks, run peer mocks with classmates, swapping the roles of interviewer and candidate and using the questions seniors told you were actually asked. Treat every mock as practice, not a verdict: each one should end with one concrete fix that you apply to the next one.

The HR round

By the HR round, the company largely believes you can do the work. This round asks a different question: can we put you in front of a client, a ward, a shop floor or a team for a year. The answers that work are specific, honest and short.

Tell me about yourself

This is nearly always the first question, and it sets the tone for everything after. Structure it as present, past, future: who you are now, what you have done that relates to this role, and what you want next. Adapt this sample:

I'm a final year B.Com student, and accounting is the part of my course I genuinely enjoy. Last tax season I interned with a small chartered accountancy firm, where I helped reconcile vendor ledgers and prepared summaries for filing. On campus, I handled sponsor payments for our college fest. I'm looking to start my career at a firm where I can grow from day to day accounting into audit work.

Keep it under two minutes and end on the future, because the future invites the next question you want to be asked. For a deeper treatment of this one question, see our guide to answering "tell me about yourself".

The questions behind the questions

  • "Why should we hire you?" asks for one clear pitch, not humility.
  • "What is your weakness?" tests self awareness. Pick a real, non fatal weakness and describe the system you use to manage it.
  • "Where do you see yourself in five years?" tests whether the role is a plan or a stopover.
  • "Are you willing to relocate or work night shifts?" deserves an honest answer now, because discovering a mismatch after joining hurts you far more than the company.
  • "Do you have any questions for us?" is a real question. Ask what a fresher's first six months look like, or how first year performance is measured. Saying "no" wastes a free chance to sound like someone who intends to stay.

Here is a sample answer to "why should we hire you", from a student interviewing for a sales trainee role:

I can't claim the most experience in this room, but I sold sponsorships for our college fest by walking into shops and hearing no a dozen times before each yes. I'm comfortable with targets, I'd rather ask an early question than make an expensive guess, and I intend to be very good at this within a year.

If a bond or service agreement comes up, read it before you accept, and raise questions politely in the HR round rather than discovering terms inside the offer letter. The method that wins any interview wins this one too: research, rehearsed stories and honest answers. Our guide to preparing for a job interview walks through that method step by step.

Running an off campus search in parallel

Campus season is short, a single offer can lock you out of everything else, and your college's visitor list is one slice of the job market, not the whole of it. An off campus search running alongside campus fixes all three problems for a few hours of effort a week.

Start with a simple tracker: a spreadsheet with company, role, date applied, status and next follow up. Apply through job portals and company career pages, watch for off campus drives and hiring challenges, and message alumni who work at companies you like, because a referral from a senior is worth many cold applications. Give the search a fixed weekly slot, say two hours on Sunday for applications and ten minutes a day for replies, so it never eats your placement prep.

Your campus preparation transfers almost fully: the same aptitude drills, the same core subjects, the same HR answers. What changes is that off campus your resume must impress strangers and software instead of a familiar placement cell, so applications have to be wide but deliberate. Handling rejection and the waiting

Rejection in campus placements is mostly structural. Companies screen by cutoff and branch, hire fixed numbers, and reject good candidates by arithmetic alone. It is not a verdict on your worth, and the students who finish the season strongest are the ones who treat each drive as data rather than judgment.

After every drive, hold a fifteen minute debrief with yourself. At which round did it end? What was the hardest question? Name one fix, and make that fix part of your preparation for the next drive. A rejection in the GD round is a communication problem to practise; a rejection in the test is a speed problem to drill. Specific losses teach; vague misery does not.

The waiting between shortlists is its own challenge. Results trickle out over days or weeks, and refreshing email every ten minutes helps nothing. Keep preparing for the next company on the calendar as though no result is coming, because mostly it is not, until suddenly it is. And step around the batchmate comparison trap: someone else's early offer says nothing about how your season ends.

If the season closes without an offer, that is a hard day and not a final one. The off campus track runs all year, and everything you built this season, the resume, the subjects, the interview reps, carries straight into it. Remember too that your first job is a beginning, not a destination; our guide to your first job as a fresher covers how to make the most of wherever you start.

A checklist for placement season

  1. A one page resume, checked by a placed senior, with every line true and explainable.
  2. Three or four core subjects revised, with a two minute spoken explanation of each project on your resume.
  3. Daily timed aptitude practice with an error log, plus one full length test every week.
  4. Ten HR answers rehearsed aloud, including "tell me about yourself" in under two minutes.
  5. One mock interview with a senior every week, recorded, with one fix taken from each session.
  6. Group discussion practice twice a week with classmates, including a topic from the news.
  7. Placement cell policies known in writing: cutoffs, the one offer rule, upgrade rules and the number of allowed attempts.
  8. A list of expected companies with last year's test and interview pattern, collected from seniors.
  9. A parallel off campus tracker with applications, referrals and follow ups.
  10. A fifteen minute debrief after every drive: which round ended it, what to fix, and one change before the next one.

Placement season rewards students who treat it like a season, not a lottery. Prepare in order, practise out loud, take every mock you can get, and keep a second track running off campus. Do that, and a process that looks chaotic from the outside becomes a set of rounds you know how to play.
