How to Ask for a Raise or a Promotion

How to build your case, pick the right moment and handle the conversation when you ask for more pay or a bigger role.

Most people wait to be noticed, hint at their ambitions, and hope the system is fair. Raises and promotions rarely work that way: they are decided in budget cycles, defended in meetings you will never attend, and given to the person whose case is easiest to repeat. The good news is that building such a case is a skill, not luck. This guide walks through how the decision actually gets made, how to prepare for it over months, what to say in the room, and what to do with every answer you can get.

How pay and promotion decisions actually get made

A raise almost never happens on the spot, because almost nobody in the building has a pot of money they can hand out that afternoon. Pay moves in cycles. Leadership fixes a total budget for increases, managers propose an amount for each person, and those proposals are reviewed, compared and approved. If your request arrives outside the cycle, the most likely outcome is a promise to revisit it when the cycle comes around.

In the United States, the cycle usually follows the performance review calendar. Your manager rates your work, then recommends an increase for you out of a merit budget, and HR checks the recommendation against a salary band, meaning the range of pay attached to your job level. Where you sit inside that band matters a lot. Someone paid low in the range has room to move; someone near the top has little, even with the same rating. That is why two people doing similar work can receive very different increases, and why "the budget was tight" sometimes really means "you are already near the top of your band."

In India, the appraisal cycle typically follows the financial year. You fill in a self-appraisal form, your manager rates you, and the ratings are then normalized across the team before increments are fixed, a step many people call the bell curve. The outcome arrives as an appraisal letter with your revised CTC, and the increment is usually expressed as a percentage. Promotions often run on a separate track, tied to grades or bands, and many companies decide them once a year or once every six months.

Promotions involve even more people than raises. Your manager usually recommends you, but the decision is made in a calibration meeting with other managers and HR, where candidates are compared against the criteria for the next level. That room has constraints you cannot see: how many openings exist, how much promotion budget survived the year, and how many people at each level the team can afford to carry.

The standard most companies apply is strict. You should already be doing much of the next-level work, consistently, before the promotion arrives. Potential counts for something, but evidence is what carries a vote in that meeting.

The practical lesson is simple. A raise or a promotion is a campaign that runs for months, and the conversation you are nervous about is only the last step of it.

Build the case over months, not the night before

Your manager's problem is not deciding whether you deserve more. Their problem is defending you in front of their own manager and HR with evidence those people can repeat. Sloppy memories lose those arguments; documents win them. So the core of your campaign is a paper trail you build quietly, starting now.

Keep a wins log

Once a week, write down anything you did that moved something the business cares about, in one line, with the outcome. Facts only, no adjectives needed. After three months you will have a list you could never rebuild from memory at appraisal time.

What counts as a win depends on your field, so here are examples from a few:

  • Sales: an account you closed, a client you talked out of leaving, a territory you opened, handover notes you wrote that the next person could actually use.
  • Nursing: charge shifts you covered, a new nurse you precepted, a checklist you introduced on the unit that cut a recurring error.
  • Accounting: an audit finding you closed, a reconciliation you automated, a month-end close you shortened by working the process differently.
  • Teaching: a module you built that other teachers adopted, coordinator duties you picked up, how your students did in the exams that matter to the school.
  • Logistics: a route change that cut delivery delays, a warehouse error rate you brought down, a peak season you planned that did not collapse.
  • Design: a redesign that reduced support tickets, a component other teams started reusing, research that changed what the product team built.
  • Software: an incident rate you reduced, a service you own that shipped on time, juniors you onboarded who became productive faster.

If you are a fresher in your first job, the wins will be smaller, and that is fine: a task you fully owned, a process note you wrote, a metric someone else started quoting because you began tracking it. The habit matters more than the size.

Translate tasks into business terms

Managers remember outcomes, not effort. "Handled customer onboarding" is a task. "Wrote the onboarding checklist every new customer now gets, which cut the questions we answer in week one" is an outcome. Write each win as what you did, what changed, and who felt it.

Money, time, risk, quality and retention are the five currencies of these conversations. Every win in your log should quietly answer one of five questions: did it make or save money, did it save time, did it reduce risk, did it improve quality, or did it keep a customer, a patient, a student or a colleague?

Take the next-level work deliberately

Pick one or two responsibilities that belong to the role above yours and do them visibly, with your manager's knowledge. Agree on it out loud: "I would like to own the monthly client review for the next two quarters." A responsibility you took on with permission is evidence; a task you quietly grabbed is a favor.

When your formal review season comes close, this log becomes your raw material. Our article on preparing for a performance review shows how to turn it into the written self-appraisal and the meeting itself.

Find out what the market pays

You need answers to two different questions. What do people with your skills and responsibilities get paid elsewhere? And where do you sit inside your own company's range? The first tells you what to ask for; the second tells you how much room the company has to say yes.

For the market question, triangulate from at least three sources: job postings that publish a range, which is increasingly common in the United States; salary comparison websites and government labor statistics; and conversations with recruiters or placement consultants, who in India often know live numbers for your role and city. Professional associations in fields like nursing and accounting publish pay guides too. Notice the details, because they change the answer: city, industry, company size, and whether you are a generalist or the person who owns the hard part.

For your position in the band, the direct route is to ask. "Can you help me understand where I sit in the range for my level?" is a fair question, and some managers will answer it. If nobody will tell you, infer from behavior: if your increases stay small while your scope grows, you are probably near the top of your band, and the honest conversation is about the next level rather than a bigger number.

One warning. Never invent a competing offer to strengthen your case; it is the kind of lie that can end a career when it surfaces, and it usually surfaces. If you want a live read on your value, the honest version is to interview. Pick your moment

Timing is about the calendar the company runs on, not about your mood on a Tuesday. The best moment is a few weeks before decisions get written down, when your manager can still shape the rating, the recommendation and the promotion case. The worst moment is after those submissions lock, when any ask becomes a request to reopen a finished process.

So find out when things actually happen. In the US, ask when review ratings and increase recommendations are submitted; the useful conversation happens before that. If the answer is "the window for this cycle has closed, so let's plan the next one," that is still a useful answer. In India, ask when self-appraisal forms go out and when increment letters land; you want your case made before the ratings are normalized, not after the letter arrives.

Layer the personal timing on top of the calendar. Good moments: right after a visible win, after you formally take on new scope, or when your role has materially changed, for example a bigger territory, a license or certification you just earned, or a system you were asked to run. Bad moments: right after layoffs, in the middle of a crisis your team caused, when your manager is two weeks into the job, or the day after your own visible mistake.

Off-cycle raises exist, but they attach to changed roles, not to changed needs. "My role now includes X, which was not part of the job when my pay was set" is an off-cycle argument. "My rent went up" is not, however true it is.

Once the moment is chosen, book the meeting properly. A short email does the job:

Hi [Manager name],

Could we set up 30 minutes in the next week or two to talk about my role and my compensation? I will bring a short summary of what I have been working on this year, so we can make it a concrete conversation.

Would Thursday afternoon suit you?

[Your name]

Notice what this email does. It announces the topic so nobody is ambushed, it promises preparation, and it is short enough that there is nothing to argue with before you are even in the room. Your manager will also prepare, which is exactly what you want: their questions will be better and the meeting will be shorter.

The conversation itself, with scripts

Keep the meeting to its word count. The structure that works is four beats: a plain opener, two or three wins in business terms, one specific ask, then silence while they respond. Ten or fifteen minutes is enough when the case has been built over months.

Thanks for making time. I want to talk about my compensation. Over the last year my role has grown: I took over [responsibility], I delivered [result], and [business outcome] changed because of it. Based on that, and on what this kind of work pays in the market, I would like to ask for an increase that brings me to [figure]. I am happy to walk you through how I got to that number.

If you are in India, the same script works with the vocabulary swapped: "I would like to discuss my increment before the appraisal cycle locks, and where I hope it lands is [figure]." If you are asking about a promotion rather than a raise, say the title: "I would like to be considered for [title] this cycle."

The questions they will ask

Expect "why do you believe you deserve this?" and answer with two or three wins and the scope change, not with years of service. Expect "where did that number come from?" and answer calmly with your market research and your band position, if you know it. Expect, sometimes, "are you saying you will leave if we say no?" and never turn that into an ultimatum. The answer is: "no, I am saying I would like to understand what would need to change, and agree on when we revisit."

After the ask, be quiet and take notes

Let them respond fully, and ask clarifying questions instead of rebutting: what does the budget look like this cycle, who else weighs in, what would you need to see from me by when. You are not there to win a debate; you are there to learn how the decision actually gets made and to leave with next steps.

A good meeting can end without an answer, and that is normal. Close by agreeing on what happens next and when, then send a short written summary the same day so the words are pinned down.

One more thing. Your manager has to carry your case into rooms you will never sit in, and they will carry it better for someone they trust. If that relationship is strained, fix it first; our guide to working well with your manager is a good place to start.

Asking for a promotion: scope, title, readiness

A promotion is not a reward for effort or hours. It is a change in scope: from doing tasks to owning outcomes, from close supervision to independence, from your own work to being responsible for other people, a system, a client relationship or a budget. When you frame your case around how hard you work, you are answering a question nobody asked.

The standard in the calibration meeting is blunt. The strongest evidence for the next level is that you are already doing parts of it, visibly and for months, without the title. Not perfectly, and not in every respect, but enough that your manager can say it out loud to strangers.

Write a readiness one-pager

Get the job description for the role above yours, or your company's career ladder if it publishes one, or simply your manager's own description of the next level. Make two columns: what that level requires, and your evidence for each requirement. If half the rows are empty, stop preparing the pitch and start filling the gaps; that page just told you exactly what to work on. If most rows are full, that page is the packet your manager can carry into the promotion meeting.

Make the ask specific

"I think I am ready for something bigger" is a feeling. "I would like us to agree on what I need to demonstrate by the end of this cycle to be promoted to senior accountant, and I would like a date when you will put me forward" is a plan. Name the title, name the level, name the cycle. Vagueness is where promotion requests go to die politely.

A script for opening that conversation:

I would like to talk about the path from [current title] to [target title]. I have already started taking on parts of that role, like [example] and [example]. I would like us to agree on what else I need to demonstrate, and on when you would be able to put me forward.

Understand why "let's revisit next cycle" happens so often. A title change usually touches grades, pay structure and headcount plans, so managers park what they cannot justify quickly. Your job is to make the justification easy: the one-pager, the named criteria, the agreed date. A polite deferral is much harder to repeat against paperwork than against a feeling.

Here is what this looks like for different people. An analyst who has run client-facing reviews alone for two quarters makes the case for associate. A junior teacher who has run a school-wide reading program makes the case for coordinator. A nurse who reliably covers charge shifts and precepts new hires makes the case for charge nurse. The pattern is identical: next-level work first, title second, evidence attached.

Handling yes, no and maybe

When the answer is yes

Confirm it in writing the same day, gratefully and specifically: the amount or the title, the effective date, and any conditions attached. In India this becomes the appraisal letter or a revised compensation letter; in the US it is usually the letter from HR with the new salary. Until the letter exists, treat the yes as agreed but not delivered, and check that the number shows up where it should.

If a verbal yes does not turn into paperwork within a reasonable time, follow up politely, once and then again. A yes that evaporates is not a yes.

When the answer is maybe

Maybe is the most common answer, and it comes in two species. A serious maybe includes specifics: what would change the answer, who decides, and when it gets revisited. A brush-off includes none of those things. Your task within two days is to convert the meeting into written criteria:

Hi [Manager name], following up on our conversation. As I understood it, you would need to see [criterion] and [criterion] before the next cycle for this to be possible, and we would sit down again in [month]. Did I get that right? If anything is off, could you correct it so I am working on the right things?

When the answer is no

Stay calm in the room. The immediate no is usually "no in this cycle," which is about the budget or the timing, not a verdict on your worth. What you need before you leave is the difference between this cycle and the next one, in specific terms. Then send the follow-up email:

Hi [Manager name], thank you for the honest conversation yesterday. I want to use the next few months well, so could you help me with two things? First, what specifically would make me a clear yes for the next cycle, in your view? Second, could we put a date in [month] on the calendar to review it together? My notes say the gaps are [X] and [Y]; happy to be corrected.

This email does three things. It turns a mood into a set of criteria, it creates a date, and it leaves a written trail that protects both of you from fuzzy memory later.

What to do after a no

Then comes the part that actually decides the outcome: you deliver on the criteria, you keep logging wins, and you show up at the agreed date. That meeting is yours by right now; do not let it slide, and do not arrive without an updated one-pager.

Then judge the follow-through honestly. If you met the criteria and the answer changed, the system works, even if it was slow. If you met the criteria and the answer did not change, ask why, in writing. If the criteria keep moving, that movement is itself an answer, and a more informative one than the original no.

You have alternatives, roughly in order of disruption. A lateral move inside the company can bring new scope, a new team and sometimes a new title without any exit. A genuine role change can justify an off-cycle conversation. And interviewing elsewhere is the only reliable way to learn what another employer would pay you; if an offer comes back, negotiating it is its own skill, and our guide to negotiating a salary offer covers that conversation. Only ever use a real offer, never a bluff, and treat any counteroffer with clear eyes rather than wounded pride.

Set your own clock too. One full cycle, done properly with documented evidence, is a fair test of the system. If two cycles pass and the criteria move twice, stop spending your best months on a case that is being declined politely, and put your energy where it compounds.

Mistakes that sink an otherwise good case

  • Ultimatums you do not mean. "Give me a raise or I resign" is only a strategy if you are truly ready to walk out, and it costs trust even when it works. Managers remember it for a long time.
  • Comparing pay with colleagues. "Priya earns more than me" puts your manager in an impossible spot, because pay details are confidential and you rarely have the full picture of someone's level, band or variable pay. It also turns the conversation into fairness between you and a coworker instead of your value. Argue from your scope and the market instead.
  • Arguing from need. Rent, EMIs, school fees and medical bills are real, and your manager may sympathize. Budgets still respond to business cases, not to pressure. Mention circumstances as context if you wish, but the case must be the work.
  • Leading with tenure. Years of service earn gratitude; scope and results earn money. "I have been here five years" is the weakest opening there is.
  • Ambushing. No cornering them at the coffee machine, no raising it in a team meeting, no sliding it in as agenda item six. A booked meeting with a prepared case respects their time and gets you a considered answer.
  • Going over your manager's head. As an opening move, it poisons the well with the exact person who has to advocate for you. If the relationship truly cannot carry this conversation, that is a different problem to solve first.
  • Asking vaguely. "I was wondering if maybe at some point there could be something" gives the other person nothing to say yes to. Decide the figure or the title before you walk in.
  • Waiting to be noticed. Visibility is part of how the mechanism works, not vanity. If nobody outside your cubicle knows what you own, nobody in the calibration meeting can vote for it.
  • Never asking. The most expensive mistake on this list. Managers hear these requests every cycle; a calm, documented ask is routine, and it is the only move that starts the process at all.

A checklist for your raise or promotion campaign

  1. Start a wins log this week: one line per win, in business terms, updated weekly.
  2. Take on one or two next-level responsibilities, with your manager's agreement and your name on them.
  3. Research the market range for your role, city and industry from at least three sources.
  4. Find out where you sit in your company's range, or infer it from how your numbers move.
  5. Learn when your company's ratings, recommendations and promotion decisions actually get made.
  6. Book the conversation a few weeks before that, with the short email, never an ambush.
  7. Bring three wins, one figure or one title, and one clear request. Then be quiet and listen.
  8. Send a written summary the same day: the agreement, or the criteria and the date.
  9. Deliver on the criteria, keep the log updated, and show up at the agreed date with an updated one-pager.
  10. If the criteria move twice, treat that as an answer, and take your evidence somewhere it will be priced properly.

You do not control the budget, the committee or the year the company is having. You control the evidence, the timing and the calm you bring to the room. That is most of what was ever in your power, and it is exactly what the people who say yes are looking for when they look at your file.
