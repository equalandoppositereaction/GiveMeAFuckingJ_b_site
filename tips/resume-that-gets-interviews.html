How to Write a Resume That Gets Interviews

What recruiters look for in the first few seconds, and how to build every section of your resume to earn the interview.

Before anyone decides to interview you, someone has to decide that your resume is worth a second look. That first look is brief and unforgiving: a recruiter skims the top of the page, your most recent job and your skills list, and makes a call. This guide walks through a resume in the order a reader meets it, so that every section answers the questions running through their head. The goal is not a beautiful document but one that survives a fast skim, holds up on a slower read, and earns you the interview.

What happens in the first few seconds

A recruiter opening your resume does not read it like a story. Their eyes land near the top left, sweep across your name and current title, drop down to your most recent job, then jump to the skills and education lines. That first pass answers a handful of quick questions:

  • Does this person's current or last job title look close to the role I am hiring for?
  • Do the skills on the page match the skills I asked for?
  • Are the dates steady, or is there a confusing gap or a pile of very short jobs?
  • Is this resume easy to read, compared with the stack around it?

Only resumes that pass that skim get a slower second read, where the content of your achievements actually matters. So your job is twofold: make the skim painless, and make sure the slower read finds something worth shortlisting.

The practical consequence is simple. The top third of page one carries more weight than the rest of the document combined, because that is where the eyes go first. Everything else in this guide follows from that fact.

Contact details: make it easy to reach you

Put your name at the top in a large, normal font, then a single line with your phone number, a professional email address, the city you live in, and a link to your professional online profile if you keep one in good shape. Designers, developers and writers should add a portfolio link. That is all a contact block needs, and it belongs in the body of the document, not tucked away in a page header.

Some details matter more than people expect. Use an email address that looks like you, such as a firstname.lastname address, not a nickname you invented in school. Check your phone number twice, because one wrong digit silently deletes every callback. Keep your voicemail set up and empty enough to take a message.

Mention your city, and if you are genuinely willing to move for the right job, add a short line saying so, such as "open to relocation". Recruiters in India filter heavily by location and notice this immediately, and hiring teams in the US do the same for office-based roles. On the other hand, leave out your full street address, date of birth, marital status and father's name. Those fields belong to an older style of biodata, they take up space, and they invite judgements that have nothing to do with your work.

The headline and summary: say what you are in one line

Directly under your contact details, state the job you are aiming for in a single headline. A nurse can write "Staff Nurse, Medical-Surgical". An accountant applying in Pune can write "Accountant: GST, Tally and month-end closing". The headline does the recruiter's first job for them, which is matching your file to their opening, and it stops them having to guess what kind of role you think you fit.

Under the headline, write a summary of two or three sentences. A good summary says what you are, gives one piece of honest proof, and names the role you want next. A bad summary is a string of adjectives. Words like "dynamic", "hardworking" and "result-oriented" appear on thousands of resumes and prove nothing, so spend the space on evidence instead.

Retail sales professional with nine years in electronics and appliance stores, the last four managing a team of twelve. Grew monthly store revenue from 40 lakh to 55 lakh rupees over two years while keeping staff turnover near the company average. Now looking for a regional sales role in consumer durables.
Final-year mechanical engineering student with two internship projects in automotive quality control. Built a checking fixture that cut inspection time on one production line by roughly a third. Seeking a graduate engineer trainee role.

If you are changing fields, say so plainly in one clause: "Primary school teacher of five years moving into instructional design, with two years of building e-learning content." One honest sentence about direction beats a paragraph about passion.

Work experience: the section that decides everything

List your jobs in reverse order, newest first. For each role give the job title, the company name, the city, and the dates as month and year, like "Mar 2023 to present". Keep the format identical for every job, because inconsistent dates are the first thing a careful reader hunts for.

Under each role, use bullets, not paragraphs. Three to five bullets for your most recent roles, two or three for older ones. Put the bullet most relevant to the job you want first, even if it was not the biggest part of that job. A recruiter matching you to an opening is scanning for their requirements in your history, so make them easy to find.

The shape of a good bullet

Every bullet should carry three things: what you did, what happened as a result, and a number where the number is honest. One workable pattern is: accomplished [a result], as measured by [a number or concrete outcome], by doing [how you did it]. You do not need all three parts in every bullet, but at least one bullet per role should show impact rather than plain activity.

Before: Responsible for handling customer complaints.
After: Resolved around 40 customer complaints a week and cut repeat complaints on one product line by getting back to every escalation within a day.

The "after" version works because it tells the reader what you were trusted with, what you moved, and how they might measure you if they hire you. The same repair works in any field:

Before: Helped with medication administration and record keeping.
After: Managed medication rounds for a 30-bed ward with zero documentation errors across two internal audits.

What if you honestly have no numbers?

Plenty of good work is hard to measure, especially in hospitality, teaching, nursing and support roles. You still have options. Count things you can verify: how many guests, students or calls you handled in a shift, how many tickets you closed, the size of your team, the number of deliveries or classrooms you covered. Use honest approximations such as "about" or "roughly", and never invent a figure.

If a number truly does not exist, describe scale instead: "for a 120-cover restaurant", "across three classrooms", "for clients in four states". Scope tells a recruiter how big your world was, and that is often exactly what they are screening for.

Dates, gaps and short stints

Recruiters read dates early, so keep them clean: month and year for the start and end of every job. If you took a gap, you do not have to apologise for it, but you can name it honestly, for example "career break for full-time caregiving, 2022 to 2023" or "relocated cities; freelancing during the transition". A named gap worries a reader far less than an empty one.

Short stints happen, especially early in a career, and hiding them is not possible anyway. Keep them, and add a one-line reason where it helps: a contract that ended, a layoff that hit the whole team, a better offer. One short job is survivable. What sinks a resume is a history where every job looks abandoned after a few months, with no explanation anywhere.

Skills, education and projects

Keep the skills section short and grouped: languages and tools in one group, domain skills in another. An accountant in India might list "Tally, GST filing, Excel (pivot tables, VLOOKUP)", and a support engineer might list "SQL, incident triage, ticketing systems". List only skills you would be comfortable being quizzed on in an interview, because that is exactly where they will surface. Leave soft skills like teamwork and communication out of the list; prove them through your bullets instead, where a reader can see them in action.

Education comes after experience once you have worked for a few years: degree, institution, city, year of graduation. If you are a fresher, education sits higher on the page, and you can include your marks if they are genuinely strong. Once you hold a degree, drop your school details; they have done their job.

Projects: the fresher's experience section

If you have little or no work history, projects are where you prove you can do the work. Treat them like jobs. Give each project a name, a one-line purpose, what you personally did, the tools you used, and the outcome. Coursework, internships, freelance work, open-source contributions, volunteer work and college festival roles all count.

Built an expense-tracking dashboard in Excel for a student survey covering 200 responses; cleaned the data and built the summary views, and the placement cell adopted the dashboard to track the batch's applications.

That bullet works because it shows tools, effort and a real outcome in one line, which is more than most fresher resumes manage.

Length, layout and file type

One page is right for students, freshers and anyone in the early years of their career. Two pages are fine when you genuinely have that much relevant history, for example a nurse with a decade across three hospitals. Never stretch one page of content into two, and never squeeze years of good work into a cramped corner of one page.

Keep the layout deliberately boring: a single column, standard section headings such as "Work Experience" and "Education", one common font, and clear, aligned dates. Be careful with design-heavy templates built from two columns, sidebars and skill graphics; they look polished and they read badly, both to busy humans and to the software that screens your file before any human sees it. Save the file as a PDF that keeps text as text, not a scan, unless the posting asks for a Word document, in which case send exactly that. Name the file clearly, such as "Priya-Sharma-Accountant-Resume.pdf", because "resume-final-2.pdf" gets lost in a folder of three hundred files.

That boring layout is not just a matter of taste. The same formatting choices decide whether screening software can read your file at all, which is covered in detail in how to get your resume past applicant tracking systems.

What to cut

Most resumes are not missing content; they are drowning in it. Cut these without hesitation:

  • Objective paragraphs that begin "seeking a challenging role in a growth-oriented organization". Your summary already does that work.
  • Photographs, unless the market or employer you are applying to expects one.
  • Personal details such as date of birth, marital status, gender or father's name.
  • Skill bars, star ratings and percentage scores for tools. "Python: 80%" tells a reader nothing.
  • "References available on request." Everyone knows; the line only fills space.
  • Hobbies, unless they connect to the job or make you memorable for a real reason.
  • Jobs from more than about twelve years back, unless they are directly relevant to the role you want.
  • Certificates you would not willingly put in front of an interviewer.

None of this is lost forever. Everything you cut belongs in a master document that you draw from when a specific opening needs it, which is the system described in how to tailor your resume to every job description.

Two strong openings, side by side

Here is how the top of a fresher resume for an accounting role in India can look:

Ananya Deshpande. Accountant: Tally, GST and bookkeeping. Pune, open to relocation. B.Com, Savitribai Phule Pune University, 2026. Summary: B.Com graduate with strong accounting papers and an internship at a Pune chartered accountancy firm, where I prepared GST returns for 15 small businesses. Comfortable with Tally, Excel and filing deadlines. Seeking an accounting role with a growing finance team.

And the top of an experienced resume for a warehouse operations role in the US:

Marcus Reed. Warehouse Operations Supervisor. Chicago, IL. Summary: Operations supervisor with eight years in distribution centers, the last three running night-shift picking for a 200,000 square foot facility. Held order accuracy above target through peak season with a team of 25 associates. Looking for an area manager role in logistics. Recent highlight: reduced picking errors by reworking how fast-moving items were slotted and retraining the two lowest-performing routes.

Notice what both have in common. The job target is stated up front, the proof is concrete, and nothing on the page needs decoding. The rest of the resume exists to support what those first few lines promise. When the interview does arrive, the next skill to work on is preparing for the job interview itself.

The final checklist

Run through this list before you send any resume:

  1. Your target job title appears in the headline or the first line of the summary.
  2. The summary contains evidence, not adjectives.
  3. Every job shows the title, company, city and month-year dates, in the same format.
  4. The first bullet under your current role is the one most relevant to the job you are applying to.
  5. At least one bullet per role shows a result, with a number where the number is honest.
  6. The skills section matches what the job description asks for, and only what you can defend.
  7. Any gap or short stint has a short, honest explanation nearby.
  8. The file is a single column with standard headings, saved as a clean PDF, or as a Word file if the posting asks for one.
  9. The file name includes your name and the role.
  10. Your phone number and email address are correct.
  11. Nothing on the page needs more than one read to understand.

A resume that passes this checklist does not guarantee anything, because hiring always involves luck and timing. What it does is remove the document itself as a reason for rejection.
