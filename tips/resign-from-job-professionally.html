How to Resign From a Job Professionally

Telling your manager, the resignation letter, notice period, handover, counter offers and leaving on good terms.

Resigning well is a skill, and it pays you back for years. The manager you give notice to today may be the person a future employer calls for a reference, or the colleague who interviews you at your next company. This guide walks through the whole exit: deciding to leave, the conversation with your manager, the resignation letter, notice periods in India and the United States, the handover, counter offers, and the mistakes that follow people long after they have moved on.

Decide what you are moving towards, not only what you are moving away from

The cleanest exits start with a direction, not just a grievance. Leaving for something, a signed offer, a new field, a plan to study further, feels and looks very different from fleeing from something. When you resign in the middle of a bad week with nothing lined up, you end up accepting the first thing that appears, which is how people hop from one mismatched job to the next.

Before you decide, write down the actual reason. If it is money, ask whether the pay problem belongs to this company or to your role and level everywhere. If it is a difficult manager, ask whether you have genuinely tried to reset that relationship, because a new manager is a lottery. If it is burnout, ask whether the next job will burn you out in exactly the same way. A staff nurse exhausted by night rotations may solve it by moving to a day-shift role in another hospital, while a nurse who wants to specialise needs a qualification first, not just a different employer. The answers point to very different exits.

If you actually like the company and the problem is one relationship, it is worth trying to repair it before you decide. Our article on working well with your manager has practical ways to reset a strained working relationship. If the attempt fails, at least you leave knowing you tried, which makes the exit story much easier to tell later.

Once you have decided, get the new offer in writing before you resign. A verbal offer, an excited recruiter call, or a screenshot of a message is not an offer. Joining dates slip, budgets freeze, positions close. The safest sequence is: written offer accepted, joining date agreed, and ideally the new employer's background check underway, then resign. If you are leaving without another job, do it with a money plan, meaning savings that cover several months of expenses and a clear idea of what you will do next.

Read your contract before you plan your last day

In India, the appointment letter usually states the notice period, commonly 30, 60, or 90 days depending on the employer, with shorter periods at startups and longer ones at large IT services and product companies. Check whether the letter mentions notice pay or buyout, whether any training costs or joining bonuses must be repaid if you leave early, and what it says about probation. If you are a fresher still inside your probation period, your notice there is often shorter, sometimes around 30 days, but only your own letter decides.

In the United States, most employment is at-will, which means you can generally leave at any time and no specific notice is legally required. The custom is two weeks, and it is a strong custom, but it comes from professionalism, not from a statute. Still, read what you signed: some roles in healthcare, finance, and management carry their own notice terms, sign-on bonus repayment clauses, or non-compete wording that affects where you can go next.

Then count your real last day on a calendar and compare it with the joining date the new employer wants. If your notice period is 90 days and they want you in 30, that gap has to be closed somehow, usually through a buyout in India or a pushed-back joining date. It is far easier to negotiate that with the new employer before you resign, not after.

Do the quiet personal preparation before you announce anything: save the personal files that genuinely belong to you, note down the personal contacts you want to keep, and finish anything you would rather not handle during a busy notice period. Do not copy anything confidential. The goal is that on your last day you are packing a bag, not scrambling.

Tell your manager first, before anyone else

Order matters more than most people expect. Your manager should hear it from you before HR hears it formally and long before the team gossips about it. Nothing burns goodwill faster than a manager learning from a colleague that their own team member is leaving. Once your manager knows, follow the company process, whether that is a form, an email to HR, or a letter copied to the HR team.

Ask for a short private meeting, ideally in person, or a video call if you work remotely. Pick a calm moment, not right after a heated review meeting and not in a corridor between tasks. Then say it simply. Something like this works:

I want to share something with you before anyone else hears it. I have decided to move on, and I have accepted an offer elsewhere. My last working day will be [date]. I did not take this decision lightly, and I want us to plan a proper handover so the team does not feel this.

Keep the conversation short and calm. It is an information conversation and a planning conversation, not a negotiation and not the moment to finally say everything you have been holding back. Expect questions: where are you going, why, what would make you stay. Decide in advance how much you want to share. You are allowed to keep the destination private; a neutral line such as "I would rather keep the details to myself for now, but I will complete my notice fully" is perfectly professional.

If your manager is the reason you are leaving, still tell them directly and keep it neutral. An account executive leaving a sales team because targets were unrealistic does not need to announce that in the resignation meeting. If your manager is travelling or unreachable, send a private message asking for time that week, and loop in HR only once your manager knows, or together with your manager if days pass with no response.

The resignation letter: short, calm, dated

The letter is a record, not an essay. Its job is to state clearly that you are resigning, from which role, and with which last working day, so nobody can later argue about dates. One short page or one short email is enough. Include your decision, your role, your last working day, a line of genuine thanks, and an offer to help with the transition. Leave out grievances, salary complaints, long explanations, and, unless you want to share it, the name of your next employer.

In India the letter is usually addressed to your reporting manager with a copy to HR, and it helps to reference the notice period clause in your appointment letter and to ask about the relieving letter. In the United States a brief email to your manager normally does the job, and some people also bring a printed letter for the file. Here is a short template you can adapt:

Dear [manager's name],

I am writing to formally resign from my position as [job title] at [company name]. As per the notice period in my appointment letter, my last working day will be [date].

Thank you for the opportunities and the support I have received here. I am committed to completing a smooth handover over my notice period.

Kindly let me know the next steps for the handover plan and the formalities for my relieving letter and full and final settlement.

Sincerely,

[your name]

[employee ID and department]

Readers in the United States can drop the relieving letter line, since that document is not part of a typical US exit, and simply ask about final pay and benefits. Keep a copy of the sent letter and note the date. The letter also fixes your last working day in writing, which protects you if anyone later disputes how much notice you served.

Notice periods: what you owe and what you are owed

India: serving it, buying it out, and collecting your paperwork

Serve the notice period properly. Those weeks are the last sample of your work this employer will see, and word travels within industries. Complete what you promised, answer messages, and do not treat the final month as a holiday with a desk.

If the full period does not fit your plans, ask about a buyout: you, or sometimes your new employer, pays the salary for the days you will not serve, and the company releases you earlier. Whether that is allowed depends on your contract and your manager's approval, so ask early, ask politely, and get the approval in writing. Some companies deduct the buyout amount from your full and final settlement instead of asking you to pay upfront. Treat a buyout as a request, not a right, so a graceful "I understand if it is not possible" keeps the conversation healthy.

Know what you are owed at the end. Your relieving letter confirms you left properly and served or settled your notice. Your experience letter states your role and your dates. Your full and final settlement covers pending salary, leave encashment, and expense reimbursements. Ask HR when each will arrive, get that confirmed in an email, and follow up politely in writing if things drag. Employers in India commonly ask for the relieving and experience letters during background verification for your next job, so file them safely. If you have a provident fund account, ask what you need to do to transfer or withdraw it, and if you have served several years, ask about gratuity eligibility too.

The United States: a courtesy, not a law

Because most US employment is at-will, notice cuts both ways. You are not legally required to give two weeks, and your employer is not legally required to accept it gracefully. Some companies end your employment the same day you resign and walk you out with your things, or pay you for the notice period instead of having you work it. That is not personal; it is simply how at-will employment works.

Practically, this means you should resign in the US assuming the exit could be immediate. Have your personal files already moved, your contacts saved, and your personal accounts unlinked from work devices before the conversation. Offer the two weeks sincerely and be prepared for any response. If your role is critical, you can offer more time, but expect the employer to decide how much of it they actually want. Before your last day, ask HR when you will receive your final paycheck and what happens to unused paid leave, since that depends on state law and company policy.

Do a handover people actually thank you for

Your handover is your last piece of work, and it is the one people discuss after you are gone. A good one protects your reputation; a careless one quietly undoes years of it. Start by writing a single handover document rather than scattering information across chat threads.

A useful handover document covers, for every project you touch: the current status, what is done and what is pending, upcoming deadlines, key contacts inside and outside the company, where the files live, and known risks or quirks. Hand over access through the proper channels, by asking IT or your manager to grant it, never by sharing your own passwords. Then offer a few working sessions to walk your successor or teammates through the document, and let them drive the keyboard while you watch. People retain far more when they click through it themselves.

Concrete examples make this real. An accountant leaving mid-year should leave the month-end close checklist annotated with the two reconciliation quirks nobody ever documented. A dispatch coordinator in logistics should note which vendor answers email and which one only picks up the phone. A teacher should leave lesson plans, notes on each class, and where parent communication stands. A designer should point the next person to the brand assets, the file naming system, and the client approvals still pending. A software engineer should document the deployment steps and the tickets that are one careless change away from breaking. None of this takes long, and all of it becomes proof that you were the professional in the room.

Agree with your manager on what you will personally finish and what you will hand off. Priorities shift during a notice period, and a written agreement prevents the last week from becoming a scramble of hurt feelings. Resist the temptation to hoard knowledge as job security. It reads badly in the moment, it fails anyway once you are gone, and it costs you the reference you will want later.

Counter offers: flattering, tempting, and rarely simple

When you resign, some employers come back with more money, a promotion, or a changed role. Understand why: your exit creates real cost and real risk for them, from recruiting effort to project deadlines, and a counter offer is sometimes cheaper than replacing you. Sometimes it is genuine appreciation arriving too late. Sometimes it is buying time. You usually cannot tell which, so do not decide on the spot.

The honest question is this: does the counter fix the reason you decided to leave, or only its price tag? If you were leaving only for money and you like everything else, staying can genuinely work, and it becomes a fresh negotiation. In that case our guide on negotiating a salary offer will help you think through what to ask for and what to get in writing. If you were leaving because of growth, workload, a manager, or the work itself, ask what specifically will change, when it will change, and whether you can have it in writing. "We will sort it out" is not a plan.

Also weigh what the resignation itself changed. Your employer now knows you were ready to leave, and some will quietly treat you differently when the next promotion or interesting project comes up. That is not certain, but it is common enough to think about. And never use a rival offer as a bluff to squeeze out a raise. If it is discovered, and it often is, you lose trust on both sides at once.

If you have decided to go, answer kindly and firmly. Something like:

Thank you, sincerely. It means a great deal that you value my work this much. This decision was never only about money, so I am going to stick with it. I will give my notice period my complete effort, and I hope we can stay in touch.

Whatever you decide, decide before the counter conversation, not during it. A choice made under a sudden number in front of your manager is rarely your best one.

What never to do in your final weeks

Do not badmouth the company, your manager, or your colleagues anywhere: not in the team, not at the farewell party, and not on review sites while emotions are high. It gets back, it always does. The exit interview deserves special care. HR records what you say, and those notes can resurface during future background checks. Give calm, specific, forward-looking feedback about systems and processes, skip the personal attacks, and never say anything you would not repeat to the person's face. For reading the room and deciding what to share with whom, our article on handling office politics is a useful companion.

Do not check out early. The final weeks are part of the job, and everyone notices who coasts. Keep your standards exactly where they were on your first day.

Do not take data, documents, code, customer lists, or decks that belong to the employer, not even "just as a backup". Taking confidential material can cost you the new job when it is discovered, and it can create a legal problem on top of that. Keep only what is genuinely yours, such as your own notes on your craft.

Do not delete anything, including files, emails, or chat threads. Deletion looks like tampering, it surfaces during handovers and audits, and it can turn a friendly exit into a hostile one. Hand things over; do not erase them. Do not announce your move on social media before your manager knows, and do not start behaving like a visitor before your last day has actually arrived.

Resigning from a job you just started

It happens more often than people admit. The role turns out different from the interview, a better offer arrives, a family situation moves you, or you simply realise the fit is wrong. Leaving a job after a few weeks or months is awkward, but it is not shameful, and handling it well costs you far less than ghosting ever will.

Once you are sure, resign promptly. Every extra week of delay costs the employer more and makes the story worse. Give whatever notice applies: in India, probation periods usually carry a shorter notice than regular employees, often around 30 days, but check your appointment letter; in the United States, two weeks is the norm. Keep the reason short and forward-looking, something like "I have accepted a role closer to the work I want to build on", rather than a list of everything wrong with the place.

A fresher in a first job faces this most sharply. If you took a campus placement or your first role at a small firm and a better opening appears, remember that the relieving letter from this first job still matters for your next background verification, so exit properly even if the stint was short. A newly qualified nurse leaving a small hospital for a larger one with a structured training programme, or a junior accountant moving to a firm with real exposure, should resign exactly as carefully as a veteran would.

Finally, plan how you will explain the short stint. A role you left within weeks can sometimes simply be left off your resume, but if a background check is likely to surface it, include it and keep a calm, one-line explanation ready. Leave in a way that keeps your references warm

Furious exits feel satisfying for a week and expensive for years. Future employers call past managers, industries are smaller than they look, and the principal at the neighbouring school, the art director who moved agencies, and the sales head who switched industries all reappear in surprising places. The way you exit decides what they say when they do.

In your final weeks, thank people individually and specifically: your manager, the teammates who helped you, the mentor who stuck their neck out for you. A short personal message beats a grand farewell speech. Send a brief goodbye note to the wider team with your personal contact details, and connect with the people you genuinely want to keep in touch with. On the last day, stay until you have actually finished: return company property, hand back access the right way, collect your documents, and settle your dues before your access disappears.

Then close the file cleanly. Keep your appointment letter, payslips, relieving letter, experience letter, and settlement details in one safe place, because background verification tends to arrive at the least convenient moment. Resigning is not the end of a working relationship; it is the last chapter you write in it, and you get to choose how that chapter reads.

Your exit checklist

Work through this in order and you will walk out with your head up, your paperwork in order, and people who can vouch for you.

  1. Decide and write down why you are leaving and what you are moving towards.
  2. Get the new offer in writing and agree on a joining date before you resign.
  3. Read your appointment letter or offer letter for the notice period, buyout terms, and any repayment clauses.
  4. Pick your last working day and check it on a calendar against your joining date.
  5. Prepare quietly: save personal files that are yours, note personal contacts, and settle loose ends.
  6. Tell your manager first, in private, with your opening line planned.
  7. Send the short resignation letter, keep a copy, and follow the company's formal process.
  8. Confirm with HR whether you will serve the notice or buy it out, and how.
  9. Write the handover document and run handover sessions with the people taking over.
  10. Ask for dates for your relieving letter, experience letter, and full and final settlement, or your final pay in the US, and follow up in writing.
  11. Answer the counter offer calmly, in writing, with a decision you made beforehand.
  12. Keep the exit interview professional: specific, forward-looking, and free of personal attacks.
  13. Return company property and hand back access through the proper channels.
  14. Thank people individually and share your personal contact details.
  15. File your paperwork safely for the background checks of tomorrow.
