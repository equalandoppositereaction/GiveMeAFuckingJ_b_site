How to Cold Email a Recruiter or Hiring Manager

Finding the right person, writing a short email that gets a reply, and following up without being annoying.

Most applications disappear into a queue, and the person who would happily hire you never hears your name. A short, well written email to a real human being changes that: someone now knows your strongest skill before your resume shows up in their list. This guide covers who to write to, how to find them and their address in fair, public ways, what the email should say, and how to follow up without becoming a nuisance.

Recruiter or hiring manager: choose the right person first

Two people can move your application: the recruiter who manages the pipeline, and the hiring manager who would be your boss. They play different roles and they respond to different emails, so decide this before you write a word.

Write to the hiring manager when a role is posted

The hiring manager decides who gets hired. In small and mid sized companies they often read the applications themselves; in bigger ones they see only what the recruiter puts in front of them. When you can identify the manager of the team with an open role, a short email can put your name in their head before the pile arrives. Apply through the official channel first, then write. Many companies need a record in their system before anyone can interview you, so treat the email as a second touch, not a substitute.

The best thing about writing to the manager is that they can answer the question every applicant actually has: what matters most in this role. Ask that, politely, and some of them will reply with genuinely useful answers you can use in the interviews later.

Write to a recruiter when you are exploring

Recruiters are the right audience when you do not have a specific role in mind, when the company is too large for managers to read raw applications, or when you want to know what is moving in your field. In-house recruiters handle one company's openings. Agency recruiters, called consultancies in India and staffing firms in the United States, fill roles across many companies at once, especially in sales, nursing, accounting, logistics, hospitality and IT services. They know which employers are growing and which searches are stuck.

Recruiters live in their inbox and triage it fast, so they reward brevity and punish vagueness. One practical note for India: much of the agency market runs its first screening on the phone or on messaging apps, and job posts usually say so. If a post invites a call or a message on a particular channel, use that channel. Cold email is one tool among several, not a religion.

One person you can usually skip: the chief executive of a large company. In a big organization the CEO will never see your email. Find the recruiter on the posting, or the manager of the team, instead.

Find the right person in fair, public ways

Cold email works when it is genuinely individual, and that starts with knowing exactly who you are writing to and using an address they chose to make public.

Find the person

  • Re-read the job post. Small companies and agencies often name the recruiter or the reporting manager right in the text.
  • Open the company page on LinkedIn and search its employees for titles like talent acquisition, talent partner or recruiter, combined with your function, such as finance or nursing.
  • For a posted role, look for the team itself. A warehouse manager job belongs to someone whose profile probably says operations manager or warehouse head at that company.
  • Check company blogs, conference speaker lists, podcasts and press interviews. People who speak publicly about their work usually welcome a competent, relevant note.
  • Check whether you share a connection who could introduce you. A warm handover beats any cold email, and asking for one is perfectly normal.

Alumni are the most underused route of all. In India, college alumni groups are full of people who will at least read a note from a junior of their institution; in the United States, university alumni networks work the same way, and many companies have employee referral groups built around them. A shared college does not entitle you to anything, but it does make a reply far more likely, so lead with it when you have it.

Find the address

Look for an address the person has published themselves: the team page of the company website, press releases that list contacts, research papers and talk slides, the person's own website, or the signature of a newsletter or forum post they wrote.

If nothing turns up, most companies use one or two repeating formats, such as firstname.lastname@company.com or a first initial followed by the surname. You can often work out the pattern from one published address of a colleague at the same company. If you are still unsure, do not fire off a volley of guesses: send your message on LinkedIn instead, or write to the careers address published on the company site. Buying scraped contact lists, blasting fifteen spelling variations, or digging out personal addresses are all bad ideas. They rarely work, and they make the professional world you are trying to join a little worse for everyone.

Subject lines that get the email opened

The person you are writing to gets a crowded inbox and triages it by subject line. Specific beats clever, every time. Put the role, the company or the context in the subject, and keep it short enough to survive a phone screen.

Applying for the Staff Nurse opening in Pune (12 years ICU experience)

Question about the Sales Manager role posted on Monday

Fresher accountant interested in your Bengaluru openings

Referred by Meera Nair for the UX designer role

All four do the same job: the reader knows what the email is about before opening it. Never send a blank subject, a bare "Hello", or anything like "Job enquiry" or "Please check my resume", which reads as a mass mail even when it is not.

The email itself: about a hundred words and one ask

Short emails get answered because they are easy to answer. Aim for roughly a hundred words in the body: who you are, why you are writing to this specific person, two lines of proof, one clear ask, and a signature with your phone number. Write in plain language, the way you would speak in a hallway, and cut every buzzword. Attach or link your resume, and make sure it is tailored to the kind of role you are writing about; if it is not, fix that first (how to tailor your resume to every job description).

What to ask for

The ask should be small, specific and easy to say yes to. Good asks: a question about the role, a short call at their convenience, whether the team is hiring at your level, or permission to send a summary they can forward. Bad asks: "please refer me", "please find me something", or anything that hands them work. You are offering to make their day easier, not harder.

Small details matter more than they should. Name your resume file properly: Ananya-Sharma-Resume.pdf, not resume_final_v3.pdf. Add a one line signature with your phone number and city, so the reader does not have to hunt for how to reach you. Send from the account you actually check, because a reply that sits unread in an old inbox is a dead reply.

Below are three full examples for three different situations. Adapt the wording to your own voice; keep the shape.

Example 1: applying to a posted role, written to the hiring manager

Subject: Question about the Operations Manager opening

Hi Priya, I saw the Operations Manager opening on your careers page and I have applied through the portal. I have run a 40 person warehouse team for the last four years, and after we redesigned the shift handover process last year, dispatch errors dropped by roughly a third. Could you share what matters most for this role in the first six months? I would like to aim at the right things from day one. My resume is attached and I can talk on any afternoon this week. Thank you for your time. Ananya Sharma, +91 98XXX XXXXX

Example 2: no specific role, written to an in-house recruiter

Subject: Experienced receivables accountant interested in your finance team

Hi James, I am an accountant with six years in receivables and month end close, currently at a mid sized manufacturer in Ohio. I am looking for my next step and your finance team is high on my list, partly because of the systems migration your CEO mentioned in last month's press release. Are you hiring at that level this quarter? My resume is here [link] and I am happy to answer any screening questions by email. If it is easier, I can send a two line summary you can forward to the relevant manager. Thanks, Daniel

Example 3: a fresher writing to an agency recruiter

Subject: Fresher electrical engineer, open to field service roles in Hyderabad

Hello Mr. Rao, I finished my B.Tech in electrical engineering this May, with a final year project on solar inverter efficiency, and I am looking for field service or maintenance roles in Hyderabad. Kavya Reddy, a senior from my college whom you placed with a pump manufacturer last year, suggested I write to you. I can join immediately and I am open to travel across Telangana and Andhra Pradesh. May I send my resume and marksheets? Thank you for considering me. Rohit Verma, +91 90XXX XXXXX

Notice what all three share: they name a person, they say why this person, they offer one or two specifics as proof, and they end with a question or a small, easy next step. None of them begs, and none of them lists twelve skills.

When and how to follow up

Inboxes are violent places. A message that gets no reply is usually buried, not rejected, so one polite follow up is expected and often works. Wait about five to seven working days, then reply in the same thread so the reader keeps the context.

Subject: Re: Question about the Operations Manager opening

Hi Priya, floating this to the top of your inbox in case it got buried. I remain very interested in the Operations Manager role and can talk whenever suits you this week or next. Ananya

Send on weekday mornings, when inboxes are being worked through, rather than late on a Friday. If the follow up also gets no reply, give it another week or two, try one short message on LinkedIn, and then move on. Two or three touches is persistence. Daily chasing is pressure, and it spends the goodwill you came to build.

Keep track of it all in a simple sheet: company, person, date sent, date to follow up, status. After a dozen emails you will not remember who is due a nudge, and a sheet turns chasing into a routine instead of a mood.

If your email produces an interview, the game changes completely: your preparation now decides everything (how to prepare for a job interview).

LinkedIn messages as an alternative

When you cannot confirm an address, or the person is clearly active on LinkedIn, a message there works just as well, sometimes better. The rules are the same, with one addition: be even shorter. A connection note is a sentence or two. A message after connecting can hold a short paragraph.

Hi Priya, I have applied for the Operations Manager role at your company. I have managed warehouse teams of about 40 for four years, with a focus on shift handovers and dispatch accuracy. May I send you a short summary of my background for that role? If the timing is wrong, no problem at all, and thank you for reading.

Do not paste the full email into a chat window, do not hit the same person on three platforms in one afternoon, and do not copy-paste one note to twenty recruiters, because recruiters talk to each other more than you think. One thoughtful message to one person, then silence while you wait. And before you start outreach, make sure your profile can survive the click, because a strong message pointing at a weak profile is a wasted message (how to optimize your LinkedIn profile for recruiters).

What not to do

  • Do not write to twenty people at once with everyone in the hidden copy field. It shows, and it reads as spam.
  • Do not open with your need. "I urgently need a job" gives the reader nothing to act on. Lead with what you bring.
  • Do not say "I can do any role, please suggest something". Pick a role, even if you are flexible underneath.
  • Do not ask about salary, leave policy or work from home in the first email. Those conversations come after interest.
  • Do not invent referrals or friendships. People verify, and one caught lie ends the thread permanently.
  • Do not send from an address like coolboy2007@. Use a plain address with your own name in it.
  • Do not chase after a clear no. Thank them, ask what would make you a stronger candidate next time, and leave the door open.
  • Do not hit send without proofreading. Spell the person's name exactly as they spell it on their profile, check the company name twice, and read the whole thing aloud once. A wrong name in the first line kills the entire note.

Before you hit send: a quick checklist

  • The right person: someone who can actually hire, screen or forward you.
  • A public, verifiable address, or a LinkedIn message instead of a guess.
  • A subject line that names the role or the context.
  • Around a hundred words, two specifics about your work, one small ask.
  • A tailored resume attached or linked, saved under a sensible file name.
  • The name spelled right, zero typos, nothing you would be embarrassed to have a colleague read.
  • A reminder in your calendar to follow up in five to seven working days if you hear nothing.
