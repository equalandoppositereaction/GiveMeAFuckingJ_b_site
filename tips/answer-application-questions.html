How to Answer Common Job Application Questions

Notice period, expected salary, work authorization, why this company: what each question is really asking, and how to answer.

Every job application form is a filter before it is a form. Some questions sort you into a pile a human will actually see, and some quietly remove you from the running before a person ever opens your resume. This article goes field by field through the questions that appear on almost every application, explains what each one is really checking, and gives you honest ways to answer.

How application forms actually screen you

When you fill in a structured field, your answer is stored as data, not as prose. Recruiters search and filter on it: everyone who said yes to relocation, everyone with at least two years of a skill, everyone whose expected salary fits the budget. Free-text boxes usually reach a human only after the structured fields have done their sorting.

Two consequences follow. First, consistency matters: your form answers will be compared with your resume and with what you say on the screening call, and mismatches raise questions you will have to answer under pressure. Second, honesty matters more than charm, because structured answers are easy to verify against documents later.

Knockout questions

A knockout question is a screening question with answers that automatically disqualify you. Recruiters configure them in hiring software for true dealbreakers: work authorization, a license the law requires, willingness to relocate, shift availability, a minimum number of years with a skill. If your answer falls outside the acceptable range, the system can reject you on the spot, often with no human review at any point.

Three practical rules follow. Read every knockout question twice before answering, because a rushed answer you cannot reverse is expensive. Never lie on one, because documents and background checks surface the truth later, and a rescinded offer is worse than a lost minute. And if you are auto-rejected for something you genuinely satisfy, send the recruiter a short, polite note with the correct information. Automation errors happen, and a human can fix what a form cannot. That note works best when it is short and specific, the way a good cold message is.

Prepare before you start filling

Most form pain comes from not having information at hand. Before you open an application, gather in one place: the month and year of every job on your resume, your appointment or offer letter if you are serving notice, recent payslips, your license and certification numbers, your earliest possible start date, and a plain-text version of your resume for paste boxes. Half-finished applications tend to stay half-finished, so start with everything ready.

Notice period

In India this question appears almost everywhere, and recruiters read it as a planning question: when can you actually join? Your contractual notice, commonly 30, 60, or 90 days, is verified later against your appointment or resignation letter, so give the real number, not the hopeful one. A joining date that slips after four interview rounds is one of the most common ways an offer falls apart.

If you have not resigned yet, give your contractual period. If you are serving notice, say how much remains and when your last working day falls. Mention the buyout option if you genuinely have one: many employers will pay to buy out part or all of your notice, or split the cost with you, and saying so can make a 90-day candidate suddenly viable. Some employers also release people early for a clean handover or a named replacement, which is worth offering to arrange.

"I am currently serving a 90-day notice period, with my last working day on November 30. My employer's policy allows buyout of up to 60 days, so I could join by the first week of November if the offer includes a buyout, and I am open to discussing how that is handled."

Freshers and people between jobs can say they are available immediately, and it is fine to name a defined delay such as final exams or a planned move. What kills credibility is vagueness. "Soon" and "flexible" are not answers.

Current and expected salary

Indian forms almost always ask both as numbers, usually in CTC, your total cost to company. Current CTC means the full annual figure: fixed pay, variable or bonus, employer provident fund, and allowances, not your in-hand monthly salary. Offers in India are routinely checked against payslips, so give the true total, with a breakdown if the text box allows one. And never enter joke placeholders like a long row of nines: these fields feed the same filters as every other field, and an absurd number is an easy auto-reject.

For expected CTC, anchor to the market rate for the role and level, not to your monthly needs. A workable pattern: a range whose bottom you would genuinely accept, placed at a sensible step above your current figure, or anchored to the market if you are switching industries, returning after a break, or moving cities. If the field is optional, leaving it blank and discussing numbers in conversation is often the stronger play. If it is mandatory and numeric, enter one you can defend, because that number will be used to sort you.

Text box with room to explain: "Current CTC is 11.5 lakh per year including fixed pay, variable bonus, and employer PF. Expected range is 14 to 16 lakh, flexible for the right role and growth path."

In the United States the same question follows different rules. A growing list of states and cities prohibits employers from asking your salary history, and many national employers have removed the field entirely, so if you encounter it, leaving it blank or writing that you prefer not to share past pay is generally safe and normal. Questions about expectations, by contrast, are still allowed nearly everywhere, so research the going range for the role and location before you type anything. Wherever a number goes, remember that a form field is an anchor, not a negotiation. The real conversation about money comes later, and it has its own method, covered in how to negotiate your salary offer.

Work authorization and visa sponsorship

This question matters most in the United States, and it usually comes in two parts: whether you are legally authorized to work in the country now, and whether you will require sponsorship now or in the future. Read the two parts separately, because many people answer the combined sentence wrong and knock themselves out of a job they were eligible for.

  • Citizens and permanent residents: no to both parts, now and in the future.
  • Already on a work visa, such as an H-1B: authorized to work now, but most likely yes for future sponsorship, since a new employer must file for your continued status.
  • Student authorization such as CPT or OPT: authorized now, and for most students yes in the future, because that authorization ends on a known date.
  • Other permits, such as a dependent's work authorization: authorized now, and the future answer depends on your specific status, so check with your school's international student office or an immigration adviser before answering.

Answer both parts truthfully even when the truth is inconvenient; saying no to future sponsorship to pass the filter leads to rescinded offers when the truth emerges. You do not need to volunteer more detail than the question asks. Timing, the employer's history with sponsorships, and your options belong in the interview, where a recruiter can also tell you whether the company sponsors for this role at all. In India and most other countries the question rarely appears on local forms. Where it does, for example on overseas postings, the same principle applies: two parts, two truthful answers.

Relocation, start date, and shift questions

Relocation questions look casual and are not. Your answer is stored, searchable, and often filtered: employers hiring for one city screen out candidates who say no, and some recruiters actively search for candidates who said yes. So think before you tick. If you are open to moving with a timeline, say so in any text box: "Willing to relocate to Bengaluru within two months of an offer" is a far stronger answer than a bare yes, because it shows you have thought about the logistics. If you genuinely cannot move, answer no honestly; a yes you cannot keep gets tested at offer stage anyway.

Start date questions deserve a real date. Give the earliest date you can actually start, accounting for your notice period, and keep it identical on the form, your resume, and your screening call. If you could start earlier for the right role, say so and say what would need to happen for that to work.

Shift and travel questions, common in nursing, hospitality, support, and logistics, are knockouts too. Night shifts and heavy travel are real requirements, not preferences the company will quietly drop. Answer for the life you actually have; a yes you cannot sustain surfaces within the first month on the job.

Why this company, and why you are leaving

Free-text questions such as "Why do you want to work here?" get skimmed, but skimmed is not the same as ignored, and specific answers stand out against a wall of generic praise. A short formula works: one sentence naming something specific about the company or role, one sentence connecting it to your skills, one sentence on what you would contribute.

"Your hospital opened a dedicated stroke unit last year, and stroke care is where I have spent the past three years of my nursing practice. The role's mix of acute monitoring and patient education matches what I do best. I would bring my certification and my precepting experience to help the unit's teaching culture."

"Reason for leaving" is where many applicants talk themselves out of a job. Keep it brief, factual, and neutral, and never criticize a past employer, however fairly. Common situations have standard, honest phrasings:

  • Layoff or restructuring: "Position was eliminated in a company-wide restructuring." That is complete, and it is understood everywhere.
  • Growth: "Looking for more responsibility in [area] than my current position offers."
  • Relocation: "Moving to [city] in [month]." State it plainly.
  • Contract ended: "Project-based contract concluded as scheduled."
  • Terminated: one honest line without detail, for example "Role was not the right fit; I have since strengthened my [skill] through [course or work]". If the form asks directly whether you were terminated, never say no.

Whatever you write, make sure it matches your resume dates and the story you tell on the phone. Recruiters compare all three, and contradictions cost more than any awkward truth.

Years of experience, and portfolio links

"How many years of hands-on experience do you have with X?" is a numeric knockout in many systems, so the number matters. Count honestly: hands-on time with the skill counts whether it came from jobs, internships, freelance work, clinical rotations, student teaching, or serious personal projects. Two habits keep you safe: be ready to explain how you arrived at the number, because interviewers do ask, and never inflate it, because verification is common and inflation is expensive later.

A fresher counting honestly: "Two years with SQL: one year across two internships where I wrote reports from the database, and one year maintaining an inventory tracker for my family's shop, which I built in my second year of college."

Do not self-reject over a small shortfall, though. Posted year requirements are frequently wish lists, and teams regularly interview people slightly under the stated number who are strong where it matters. If you meet the core of the role, apply and answer with your real count.

Portfolio and project links get a quick scan from someone deciding whether to look closer. Make the link work from a phone, put your best and most relevant work first, and label each piece with one line: what you did, what tools you used, what the result was. A design profile, a code repository, a writing samples page, or a folder of lesson plans all work. A dead link, a login wall, or a wall of unrelated work is worse than no link at all.

The small fields people answer carelessly

A few low-effort fields do more damage than their size suggests. "How did you hear about us?" is worth answering precisely, because naming the referral or the event you attended helps the recruiter connect your application to the person who flagged it. Location fields are used to filter by distance, so give the city you can actually work from, not the city where your parents live. "Are you willing to complete a background check?" means yes for almost everyone, and hesitation reads worse than the check itself. And when a form asks for references, ask your references before you type their names, not after the recruiter calls them.

Paste boxes are their own trap. When a form gives you a big text box for your resume, paste a plain-text version rather than formatted text, then read it back once, because bullet symbols and columns often scramble in transit. Keep the formatting for the PDF you attach, and keep the paste clean.

Final checklist before you hit submit

  • Knockout questions read twice, answered truthfully, and false auto-rejections challenged with a short email.
  • Notice period: the real contractual number, with buyout or early-release options if they exist.
  • Salary: full current CTC or base, a defensible expected range, no placeholder junk, blank where the field is optional or where the law says it should not be there.
  • Work authorization: "now" and "in the future" answered as two separate questions.
  • Relocation, shifts, and travel: answers for the life you actually have, with timelines where possible.
  • Free-text answers: specific to this employer, consistent with your resume, no badmouthing.
  • Portfolio links tested on a phone, best work first.
  • A copy of everything submitted, saved for the screening call.

Forms filter you before any human judges your writing, so pair this with the parts of an application a person will eventually read: how to write a cover letter that actually gets read.
