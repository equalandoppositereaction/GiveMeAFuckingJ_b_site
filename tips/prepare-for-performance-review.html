How to Prepare for Your Performance Review

Writing your self-assessment, gathering evidence of your work, taking feedback well and setting goals for the next cycle.

A performance review is one of the few meetings where somebody writes down, in the company's permanent records, what they think of your work. That written version shapes your increment, your bonus, your promotion case and the projects you are trusted with next year. Preparing for it properly is not office politics. It is making sure the record reflects the work you actually did.

What your review actually decides

In most companies the review decides much more than a pat on the back. In India, the annual appraisal, usually timed around the financial year, feeds straight into your increment letter, your variable pay and your rating band. In the United States, it typically drives the merit increase, the year end bonus and whether your name goes into a promotion packet.

It also decides quieter things. Managers use ratings to choose who gets the interesting projects, who gets groomed for the next level and who stays on the list when budgets tighten. For a fresher, the first review often decides whether probation converts into a confirmed job.

Finally, the review creates a paper trail. Your self-assessment and your manager's comments sit in your employee file, and a future manager or an HR team can read them when you change teams or apply for an internal role. Write everything with that audience in mind.

Keep a record of your work all year

The habit that separates prepared people from everyone else is a running log of your work, sometimes called a brag document or a win file. It takes ten minutes at the end of each week. You write down what you finished, what changed because of it and who noticed.

Do not trust your memory to do this job. By appraisal season you will remember the last two months clearly and the previous ten as a blur, which is exactly when the form asks about the whole year. A log fixes that, and it doubles as ready material for your resume whenever you next look for a job.

Worth capturing:

  • Work you finished, with rough dates, including small items that added up over the year.
  • Numbers that moved because of you: orders booked, tickets closed, patients handled, deliveries on time, errors caught before they reached a client.
  • Praise that arrived in writing, a forwarded client email, a thank you message from another team.
  • Problems you solved that nobody assigned to you.
  • New skills, certifications and tools learned, and training you passed on to others.
  • Extra duties: covering for a colleague, mentoring an intern, running the rota, joining a committee.

A log does not need to be fancy. Two lines per entry is enough, as long as the result is in there. Here is how a school teacher's entries might look:

February: built a new worksheet set for class 8 science. Two other teachers have adopted it for their sections.

March: ran the class 9 exam coordination while the science head was on leave. No schedule slips.

May: finished the district training on inclusive classrooms and shared the notes with the department.

Keep the log private, in a personal document you control, and store only what you are allowed to keep. Never copy confidential client or company data into a personal file. Note the fact and where the proof lives instead.

Write the self-assessment

Before you type a word

Read three things first: the form itself, so you answer every question actually asked; the goals or KRAs that were set at the start of the cycle; and your job description. Many forms ask about each competency separately, so one great story cannot cover a whole form. If the instructions are vague, ask your manager how long and how detailed they want it.

The pattern that works

Every strong answer follows the same shape: what you did, in what context, what happened as a result, and what it shows about you. Two or three sentences are enough for each. Write in plain first person, cover the whole review period, and put one concrete fact behind every claim.

The most common failure is writing duties instead of results. A duty is what anyone in the seat would do. A result is what you did with the seat.

Weak: responsible for handling customer complaints.

Stronger: handled complaints for the north zone, cleared a backlog of around 40 pending cases and wrote a short playbook that new agents now use in onboarding.

Example lines you can adapt

These are samples to show the shape, not words to copy. Swap in your own work, your own numbers and your own context.

Software, fresher: closed 60 tickets in my first year and fixed the login bug that had been open the longest. My lead now assigns frontend work to me directly.

Sales: grew my territory's monthly orders from 8,000 to 9,500 by adding 12 dealer accounts and reviving three dormant ones.

Nursing: precepted two new nurses through orientation and was asked to take a third, which the unit usually reserves for senior staff.

Accounting: brought the month-end close down from six working days to four by moving vendor reconciliation to a weekly schedule.

Teaching: my class 10 section moved up two bands in the board practice exams, and I ran weekend sessions for 15 students who had fallen behind.

Logistics: kept on-time delivery above target through the festive peak by replanning two routes and adding a backup packing vendor.

Design: rebuilt the onboarding screens after user tests, and support questions about sign up dropped noticeably the following quarter.

Be honest about what went wrong

Nearly every form asks about challenges, misses or areas to improve. Leaving it blank, or writing that there were none, reads as either hiding something or lacking self awareness. Pick a real miss, own it in one sentence and show what you changed as a result.

I underestimated how long vendor onboarding would take in the first quarter, which pushed two launches back. Since then I add a buffer week to any plan that depends on outside parties, and the last three launches have held their dates.

Gather your evidence

Evidence turns opinions into facts, and it takes one focused hour. Before you write a single answer, collect your win file, the praise folder in your inbox, your dashboard or CRM numbers, production or sales reports, chat messages where someone thanked you, training certificates and last year's review.

Then match evidence to the form, competency by competency. If the form asks about communication, the proof might be the onboarding guide you wrote or the client updates your manager keeps forwarding. If it asks about ownership, the proof is the backlog you cleared that was nobody's job. Aim for one or two specifics per question rather than a wall of examples.

Good evidence usually falls into four kinds:

  • Numbers you already have legitimate access to: dashboards, reports, your own tracker.
  • Written praise: emails, chat messages, client notes.
  • Things you made: guides, decks, checklists, lesson plans, code, templates.
  • People who saw it: a colleague or stakeholder your manager can ask.

One rule keeps this clean: use only data you are entitled to see, and never move confidential material into a personal document. Note the number and where it lives. A claim your manager can verify in a minute is worth more than a paragraph of adjectives.

Handle your self rating with care

Many forms, especially in Indian IT services and banking, ask you to rate yourself on a five point scale before your manager rates you. Read the written description of every level first. Top ratings usually describe work that influenced people beyond your own team, not merely good work.

Rate yourself at the level the evidence supports, which for a solid year is usually somewhere in the upper middle. Inflating to the top can make you look out of touch, because your manager compares your rating against their own and against your peers. Underselling to sound humble hurts too: ratings drive increments, and nobody else will argue your number up for you.

Whatever you pick, justify it instead of letting the number stand alone.

I have rated myself 4. I met every committed goal, took on the migration project that was not in my original goals and mentored two juniors through their first release. I have held back from a 5 because I read the top rating as work that changes how the whole team operates, and that is what I want to aim at next year.

If your company fits ratings to a curve, ask your manager early how the distribution works. In a forced curve, good work can still land a middle rating, and it is better to know the rules before you write anything.

Take feedback well, even the hard parts

In the meeting

When criticism comes, your only job in the room is to understand it, not to defeat it. Take notes, keep your voice level and ask for specifics until you could explain the pattern to a stranger. What people remember afterwards is not the criticism but how you took it.

Thank you for being direct. Can you walk me through an example where this showed up, so I know exactly what to change?

I had not seen it that way. Give me a few days to sit with it and I will come back with what I am doing about it.

What would good look like? If this comes up again next cycle, what should I have done differently?

When the criticism is unfair or simply wrong

Do not argue the point in the meeting, even when you are certain it is mistaken. Facts delivered in frustration sound like excuses. Instead, write a short, calm follow-up afterwards with the facts, framed like a colleague solving a problem rather than a lawyer building a case. If written English is not your strongest suit, our article on professional email etiquette will help the tone land right.

Turn feedback into action

Every piece of criticism should leave the meeting attached to a next step, even a small one. Feedback that visibly changes something within a month is the cheapest reputation repair there is. Feedback you nod at and quietly ignore becomes the first line of next year's review.

Set goals and talk about the next role

The last stretch of the meeting is where next year gets decided, so do not let it run out of clock. Agree on two or three goals specific enough to be checked later. Improve communication is not a goal. Send a weekly status note on the migration and present the quarterly update to the leadership team is.

Next cycle I want to own the merchant onboarding flow end to end and lift its completion rate. I also want to finish the certification that my role's next level lists as required.

I want to move from executing audits to planning them. Could I draft the plan for one small client next quarter, with your review before it goes out?

Then ask directly about the next role: what separates your current level from the one above it, what work would demonstrate it, and what timeline is realistic. Managers respect a direct question far more than a year of hoping they will notice on their own.

If your goal is money rather than title, treat the review as the opening move rather than the negotiation itself. It builds the rating that a raise conversation will stand on. When that moment comes, read our guide on how to ask for a raise or promotion.

Questions worth asking your manager

A review is also your best scheduled chance to learn how your manager thinks. Ask a few real questions and write down the answers.

  • Of the work coming up next quarter, what matters most to you?
  • What is the gap between my current level and the next one here?
  • Is there anything I do that makes your job harder, even in a small way?
  • How do you prefer to receive bad news from me: early and rough, or polished and late?
  • Which one skill should I build this year that would help the team most?

The answers tell you how you are actually being measured, which is often not exactly what the form says. If the relationship needs more than one meeting a year to work, our article on working well with your manager goes much deeper.

When the review goes badly

A bad rating stings, and it helps to know it happens to good workers. A forced curve, a rough year for the whole team, or a manager who only noticed your last two months can all drag a rating down. What you do in the weeks after matters far more than the meeting itself.

In the room, stay professional. Ask what specifically would have moved the rating up, what would move it up next time, and whether there is an improvement plan with written expectations and checkpoints. Afterwards, send a short summary email so you and your manager share the same record of what was said.

If you are put on a formal improvement plan, take it seriously instead of treating it as an insult. Get the expectations in writing, hit every checkpoint and ask for a mid point review. Many people clear these plans and rebuild their standing. Some use the pressure to finally focus a job search. Both outcomes are respectable.

If the review is genuinely unfair, build a factual case and raise it calmly, first with your manager and then with HR if that goes nowhere. This is where your evidence folder pays off twice, because it is also raw material for your resume if you decide the situation is not fixable. Testing the market while still employed is a normal, sensible move, and our guide to writing a resume that gets interviews starts from work you have already done.

What not to do: quit in the room, coast out of resentment, or vent widely to colleagues. The months after a bad review are watched closely, and a calm, improving stretch is the strongest reply you can make.

A checklist for the weeks around your review

  • A month before: read your win file for the whole year, then re-read the form, last year's goals and your job description. Ask your manager about format, length and how the rating scale works.
  • Two weeks before: draft the self-assessment. One fact behind every claim, every box filled, including the hard ones. Choose your self ratings and justify each one.
  • A week before: collect missing evidence, pick the two or three points you most want heard, and prepare your questions.
  • In the meeting: lead with your best results, listen to criticism without arguing, take notes, and leave with agreed goals and next steps.
  • After: send a short summary email with the agreed goals, put every commitment in your calendar, and write the first entries of next year's win file.

Prepared this way, a review feels less like a verdict and more like a working meeting about your year. The work stays the same, but the record it leaves behind finally matches what you did.
