How to Negotiate Your Salary Offer

How to research your market rate, when to name a number, and how to ask for more without risking the offer.

You did the hard part: the interviews went well and an offer is in front of you. Before you sign, remember that this conversation sets the base for everything that follows, because raises, bonuses and future offers are usually calculated from your current pay. Negotiating well is a skill with a method: know the numbers, judge the whole package, ask once, clearly and kindly.

Understand every part of the offer first

The headline number is never the whole offer. Before you react to any figure, separate the money that reaches your bank account from the money printed on paper, because offers are built differently in India and the United States.

How pay is quoted in India: CTC, LPA and in-hand

Indian offers usually quote CTC, the cost to company, often expressed in lakhs per annum (LPA), for example 12 LPA. CTC counts everything the employer spends on you in a year, and a good chunk of it never reaches your account. A typical break-up includes basic salary, house rent allowance, other allowances, the employer's contribution to your provident fund, a gratuity provision, insurance premiums, and variable pay or performance bonus. Your in-hand salary is what remains after income tax, your own provident fund contribution, professional tax, and any variable amount that has not been paid yet.

This is why two offers with the same CTC can pay very different amounts each month. An offer of 12 LPA with 10 fixed is usually worth more than one with 8 fixed and 4 variable, because variable pay typically depends on targets, company performance or your rating, and it can arrive late, in part, or not at all. When an offer arrives, ask HR for the full break-up in writing: how much is fixed, what the variable depends on and when it is paid, whether there is a joining bonus and whether you must repay it if you leave early, and what the probation and notice period terms are. If you currently hold a job, also ask whether the new employer will cover your notice period buyout; many will contribute if asked directly, and almost none offer it on their own.

How pay is quoted in the United States: base, bonus and equity

US offers lead with a base salary, an annual figure and the guaranteed part. Many roles add an annual bonus quoted as a target percentage of base, and often marked discretionary, which means the actual payout can fall short of the target. Some companies add equity: RSUs, shares that vest on a schedule, usually over several years. A 48,000 dollar grant vesting over four years pays 12,000 dollars a year, not 48,000 in your first year. Benefits complete the offer: health insurance and how much of the premium you pay, retirement contributions, and paid time off.

So compare offers on a rough annual total: base, plus a realistic bonus rather than the best case, plus the yearly value of equity, plus the benefits that matter to you. One-time money, such as a signing bonus, is real money but does not repeat, so it should never close a gap that recurs every year.

Research your market rate before anyone mentions a number

You cannot judge 9 LPA or 85,000 dollars without knowing what your role pays in your city, industry and situation, so build that picture before the first salary conversation, not after. Useful sources: job postings that publish ranges, public salary databases for your field, professional communities where people at your level talk plainly, people one step ahead of you in your network, and any published pay scales for your profession, such as the standard scales used in government, teaching and nursing roles.

Weight recent information for your city most heavily, because the same title pays very differently across cities, and across industries hiring for the same skills. Then set three private numbers: a walk-away figure below which you decline, a target you will actually ask for, and a stretch slightly above it, so that meeting in the middle still lands you at target. Keep the research to yourself; it is for judging offers, not for quoting at HR as proof.

While you research, also gather the evidence that moves you toward the top of a range. Certifications and licenses the role explicitly asks for, scarce skills the posting mentions more than once, experience with the exact systems, tools or customer types the team works with, and measurable results from your current or past work all justify the upper half of a band. Write down two or three such facts and keep them ready, because they become the reason in your counter offer later.

When salary comes up, and how to answer "expected salary"

Pay can surface at several points: a field on the application form, the recruiter's first call, the HR round in India with its "what is your expected CTC?", and the offer itself. The later the number is exchanged, the stronger your position, because every round you pass makes the company more invested in you. Early questions deserve soft answers.

On application forms that force a figure, give a wide range you would genuinely accept; how to answer common job application questions covers that field specifically. In live conversations, turn the question around once, and if you are pressed, give a researched range with your target near the bottom:

"I'm targeting 9 to 11 LPA on fixed pay, depending on the overall structure. I'm flexible for the right role. May I ask what range has been budgeted for this position?"
"Based on my research for this role in this market, I'd expect something in the 85,000 to 95,000 dollar range for base, plus bonus. I'm flexible depending on the full package. What range do you have in mind?"

Both answers do the same two things: they quote a range you would honestly accept, and they end by handing the question back, which occasionally puts the company's budget on the table first. Never quote a number you would resent accepting, because offers have a way of landing exactly there.

A related question deserves its own plan: "what do you currently make?" In India, candidates are usually expected to share their current CTC, and hiding it risks trouble at background verification, so if you are asked directly, answer accurately and then pivot immediately to what you are asking for and why. Where you genuinely have a choice, pivot instead: "I'd rather keep the focus on this role and what my expectations are for it." The past price of your labor is not the same thing as the current market price for it.

Judge the whole offer, not only the number

When the offer arrives, score it as a package before you score the top line. Work through this list:

  • Fixed versus at-risk pay. Guaranteed money counts for more than promised money, in every country.
  • One-time money. Joining or signing bonuses and relocation help are welcome, but they do not repeat next year.
  • Insurance and retirement. What the health cover includes, what the employer puts into provident fund or a retirement plan, and whether your family is covered.
  • Leave and hours. Paid leave, shift expectations, overtime norms, travel days.
  • Location. Remote days, commute time and cost, relocation support.
  • Growth. When your first review lands, the appraisal cycle, the title, the learning budget, and how people who held this role before you have progressed.
  • Security. Probation terms, notice period, and how stable the business itself looks.

A slightly lower offer with mostly fixed pay, real health cover and a review at six months can beat a bigger CTC that is one third variable and reviews nobody until year two. Do that arithmetic before you negotiate, because it changes what you ask for: if the fixed portion is thin, your first ask is more fixed, not more total.

If this is your first offer, weight growth and learning heavily in that arithmetic. The gap between a good first team and a mediocre one compounds over years, in a way a small difference in starting pay does not.

How to make a counter offer

Most offers contain some room, and a polite, well-reasoned counter is a normal part of hiring, not an insult. Wait for the full written offer rather than a verbal number. Then, in one short message or call: thank them, confirm you want the job, make one specific ask with a reason, and offer a fallback. Anchor the ask to your value and the market, never to your personal bills. Ask slightly above your target, so that a compromise still gets you there. Email is usually best for the first counter, because it lets you write carefully and gives them time to check what is possible.

"Thank you for the offer for the Accounts Executive position. I'm pleased to accept in principle: the team and the responsibilities are a strong fit for me. On compensation, the offer quotes a 10.5 LPA CTC with 8.5 LPA fixed. Based on the market for this role and my experience running full books independently, I was expecting fixed pay closer to 9.5 LPA. If that works, I'm ready to sign this week. If the fixed portion is capped, could we discuss a joining bonus or a salary review at six months instead?"
"Thank you for the offer; I'm excited about the role and the team. Before signing, I'd like to discuss base salary. Comparable roles in this market for my level of experience are closer to 92,000 dollars than the 84,000 offered, and my track record running paid campaigns for accounts of this size supports that. If base is constrained, I'd welcome a conversation about a signing bonus or a formal review after six months. Is there flexibility on any of these?"

The pattern in both: enthusiasm, one number, one reason, one fallback. You are not rejecting the offer; you are showing them how to get to yes.

Base is not the only lever, and sometimes it is the weakest one. Titles, start date, remote days, extra leave, a learning budget, an earlier review, relocation support and one-time bonuses are all negotiable, and several of them cost the employer almost nothing.

Handling pushback

"This is our best offer." "There's not much room." "We have a fixed band for this role." You will hear at least one of these, and sometimes it is simply true. Stay warm and probe once more, without ultimatums:

"I understand, and I appreciate the straight answer. Since base looks fixed, could we look at one-time options: a joining or signing bonus, or a review date at six months? If neither is possible, I'd ask for the review date in writing, and I'll gladly accept today."

Three outcomes are possible: they find room, they offer a smaller concession, which happens often, or they genuinely cannot move. Decide your walk-away number before the call, so the moment never catches you unprepared. If their final number clears it, accept gracefully; a company that explained its limits honestly has not mistreated you. Never accept while planning to keep pushing, and never invent a competing offer, because a discovered bluff ends far more than one negotiation.

Pace matters as much as words. After you state your ask, stop talking and let the silence sit; filling it with a nervous discount is the most common self-inflicted wound in these conversations. Keep the whole exchange slow, too. A company that pressures you to decide the same day is telling you something about how it will treat you later, and asking for two or three days to think is always reasonable.

If you do hold a live process elsewhere, that is real leverage, and it exists only if you kept other applications moving: how many jobs you should apply to explains the volume that makes this possible.

Get the final deal in writing

Nothing you negotiated exists until it appears in the offer letter or a written confirmation. In India, ask for the complete break-up: the fixed and variable split, the conditions under which variable is paid, the joining bonus and any repayment clause, notice period buyout reimbursement if it was promised, relocation, insurance, probation and notice period, and any promised review date. In the US, the letter should state base, the bonus target and whether it is discretionary, the equity grant and its vesting schedule, and the start date, with the benefits summary alongside.

If a promise was made on a call, send a short confirmation email the same day: "Thank you for the call. Confirming what we discussed: ..." A plain email like that is a legitimate record. Then, and only then, resign from your current job, and in India build your notice period into the joining date you agree to.

When not to negotiate

The skill includes knowing when to leave it alone. Keep negotiation minimal or skip it entirely when:

  • The scale is genuinely fixed. Government roles, unionized positions and many campus offers with standard slabs will not move on pay, though joining dates and locations sometimes will.
  • The offer already clears your stretch number and was presented as final with clear reasoning.
  • The role is a deliberate step into a new field. Taking a modest first salary to enter nursing, teaching or a new industry can be a rational trade, once, with a plan for the next move.
  • Winning would require a threat you will not carry out. If you cannot walk away and hold no alternative, you can still ask for small things, but be clear about which asks are needs and which are wants.

And once you have accepted, stop. Reopening a signed deal burns trust you will need in that building. Your next negotiation is your first appraisal.

Your negotiation checklist

  1. Get the full break-up before judging any offer: fixed, variable, bonus, equity, benefits.
  2. Set your walk-away, target and stretch numbers before the first money conversation.
  3. Delay naming a number as long as you politely can; when pressed, give a real range and hand the question back.
  4. Counter once: enthusiasm, one number, one reason, one fallback.
  5. On pushback, ask about one-time money, review dates and non-cash levers.
  6. Confirm every promise in the offer letter or a same-day email before you resign.
  7. Know when to accept as-is, and stop once you have said yes.

Pay is set by people in rooms, and you are allowed in the room. Prepare the numbers, ask plainly, and let them tell you what is possible.
