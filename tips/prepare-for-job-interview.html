How to Prepare for a Job Interview

A step by step plan for the week before an interview: research, stories, practice, and the questions to ask.

Applications go into systems and disappear. Interviews are different: for half an hour or an hour you are in the room, and none of that time has to be left to chance. This article gives you a plan for the week before, from research and stories to logistics and the questions you should ask, so the interview feels like a conversation you have already rehearsed.

The week before: a day by day plan

Assume seven days. If you have fewer, compress: merge the company research and job description days, and never cut the practice out loud day. The order matters, because each step feeds the next.

  1. Day 7: confirm and frame. Reply to the invitation, confirm the time and format, and note who you are meeting and for how long. Re-read the job description twice and print it.
  2. Day 6: map yourself onto the role. For each requirement in the description, find one piece of proof from your past. This is where your story bank starts (see below).
  3. Day 5: research the company. Work through the layers in the next section, and write down the three to five questions the research creates.
  4. Day 4: prepare for the hard part. Whatever the technical, case, aptitude or portfolio round looks like, spend the session on it. This is the longest single block of the week.
  5. Day 3: practise out loud. Speak your answers, record them, listen, and cut them down. Include "tell me about yourself" (how to answer "tell me about yourself").
  6. Day 2: logistics. Test the technology or plan the route, choose and prepare your clothes, print resume copies, and charge everything that charges.
  7. Day 1: light review only. Skim your stories and your questions, check the logistics once, and go to bed at a normal hour. Cramming at midnight buys nothing and costs you sleep.

Research the company until you have real questions

Research has four layers, and each one feeds your answers differently.

  • The basics. What the company sells or does, who its customers are, how big it is, and where it operates. You would be surprised how many candidates cannot describe what the employer actually does by the second interview.
  • The present. Recent news: product launches, new locations, funding, leadership changes, expansions. The company's own website news section and its LinkedIn page are the fastest sources.
  • The inside. Employee reviews on public job sites show how the company treats staff and what its interview process was like for others. Read for patterns, not for individual grudges.
  • The people. Look up your interviewers: their role, their path, and anything they have written or posted recently. A shared reference point is a normal, honest way to connect.

Research only becomes useful when it turns into words you can say. Convert what you learn into questions: how a new facility changes the team's work, or what the interviewer personally thinks of the direction the company is taking. If you are a fresher in India preparing for campus or early hiring, add the company's products and clients, and the structure of its hiring rounds, to this list.

Read the job description like a checklist

Copy the description into a document and split it into three groups: must have, nice to have, and jargon you do not recognize. For every must have, write one concrete example from your own work that proves it. For every nice to have, write down the closest transferable thing you have done. For the jargon, look it up; half of it is industry vocabulary for things you already know under different names.

Then reread the description for mood. The wording tells you what problem the team has: a post that stresses "wearing many hats" is short handed, one that stresses "process" is drowning in chaos, one that stresses "stakeholders" has a communication problem. You will not always be right, but walking in with a theory about the role's biggest problem gives your answers a spine. If your resume does not yet line up with this description, fix it before the interview, because interviewers often read it fresh right before they meet you (how to write a resume that gets interviews).

Build your story bank with the STAR method

Most interviews lean on behavioral questions: tell me about a time you handled a conflict, missed a deadline, led a team, fixed something broken. The reliable way to answer is STAR: the Situation you were in, the Task you were responsible for, the Actions you personally took, and the Result. Interviewers ask because past behavior is the cheapest honest preview of future behavior.

Before the interview, prepare eight to ten short stories covering the usual themes: a proud achievement, a conflict you resolved, a mistake you made and fixed, a time you led without authority, a deadline under pressure, a difficult customer or patient or student, and something you learned fast. Write each one in four lines, then apply the timing rule: situation and task together take two or three sentences, and most of the airtime goes to actions and results. Two minutes is the ceiling. One story can answer many questions, so a bank of ten covers almost anything they throw at you.

Freshers, take note: your stories do not need to come from a job. Internships, final year projects, college festivals, volunteer work and team sports all count, as long as you personally did something and can say what changed.

Question: "Tell me about a time you worked under real pressure."

Situation: "Last year our ICU ran two nurses short through a festival week, with new admissions arriving every evening." Task: "As the senior nurse on the evening shift, I had to keep patient care safe without waiting for extra staff." Action: "I split the ward by patient acuity instead of by bed count, kept the critical patients in one zone with a junior nurse I coached through the checks myself, and asked the duty manager for one floating aide. I also compressed the shift handover onto a single sheet so the night team could take over in two minutes." Result: "We went the whole week with no missed medication rounds and no escalations, and the acuity based zoning was later reused during other staffing gaps. The lesson stuck with me: redesign the work before asking for more people."

Prepare for the technical, case or aptitude round

Ask the recruiter in advance what the rounds will look like; it is a normal question, and the answer changes how you spend day 4. Then prepare for the format you actually face.

  • Software and data roles. Practise on problems similar to the company's style, and practise explaining your thinking while you work, because interviewers grade the thinking as much as the answer. Be ready to walk through your past projects piece by piece and defend every choice.
  • Case interviews in consulting, marketing and operations. Learn a basic structure for breaking a problem down, clarify the question before you answer, and practise thinking out loud; silence is the enemy in a case.
  • Portfolio and teaching roles. Prepare a walkthrough of two or three pieces of work: the brief, your choices, the result. A demo lesson or a design review should be rehearsed, not improvised.
  • Aptitude and HR rounds, common in Indian hiring, especially for freshers. Large employers often open with a timed test of quant, logic and English, continue with a technical round on degree fundamentals and projects, and close with an HR round covering relocation, salary expectations and availability. Revise your core subjects and your final year project, and know your notice period exactly: never misstate it, because it surfaces again at the offer and verification stage.

Practise out loud, not in your head

Reading your answers silently is the most common preparation mistake. Sentences that flow in your head collapse in your mouth, because speaking is a physical skill. So speak. Say the answers out loud, time them, record them on your phone and listen back. The first recording is always humbling, and that is the point. Listen for fillers, for answers that wander, and for stories that never reach a result.

Then do at least one mock interview with a friend, a family member, or your college's placement cell if you are a student. Ask them to interrupt with follow up questions the way real interviewers do: "why did you do that?", "what would you do differently?". Do not memorize scripts word for word. Memorize the bones instead: your first sentence, your three points, and your last sentence. Improvised flesh on rehearsed bones sounds natural; anything else sounds like a crash.

Get the logistics boring

Every year, good candidates lose interviews to preventable problems. The goal is logistics so settled that nothing on the day requires thought.

If the interview is online

  • Test the platform a day early with a friend: camera, microphone, headphones, and screen sharing if you might need it.
  • Put the camera at eye level by raising the laptop, and face a window or a lamp so light falls on your face; never sit with a bright window behind you.
  • Choose a quiet room, silence your phone, and close every other program. Tell the household when you will be busy.
  • Keep a few bullet points on paper beside the camera, never a script on the same screen; eyes flicking to another tab are visible.
  • Have backups: a charged phone with the meeting link, and your phone's hotspot if the internet at home is unreliable. Join five minutes early.
  • If the technology fails anyway, stay calm, message the recruiter immediately, and do not panic; interviewers have seen it all.

If the interview is in person

  • Plan the route and add a buffer for traffic; aim to arrive about fifteen minutes early, and wait nearby if you are too early.
  • Carry identity documents, printed copies of your resume, and any certificates the company asked for, which is common in India.
  • Take a notebook and pen. Eat something beforehand and drink water; a hungry brain is not an interview asset.

Questions worth asking them

When the interviewer asks "do you have any questions for us?", the answer is always yes. Bring five or six written questions; you will only need two or three, and some get answered along the way. Skip anything a search would tell you, because it signals you did none.

  • "What does success in this role look like in the first six months?"
  • "Why is the position open?"
  • "How is the team structured, and who would I work with most closely?"
  • "What is the hardest part of this job?"
  • "What did the last person in this role do well, and what would you want done differently?"
  • "Is there anything about my background that gives you hesitation? I would rather clear it up now."
  • "What are the next steps in the process, and what is your timeline?"

The hesitation question takes nerve and it works: it surfaces the doubt that would otherwise sink you silently, and it gives you the chance to answer it while you are sitting right there.

On the day itself

Eat, drink water, and arrive or log in early. Phone silent. Then hold one simple frame: an interview is a conversation where you are also evaluating them. Listen to each question fully before answering, and if a question is hard, pause; taking a moment to think reads as careful, not slow. Answer what was actually asked, not the question you prepared for, and if you do not know something, say so and reason aloud instead of faking it. Interviewers respect honesty far more than a confident wrong answer.

After the interview

Within a day, send a short thank you email to each interviewer, in the same thread as your existing conversation. Keep it to a few lines and make it specific.

Thank you for the conversation today about the Operations Manager role. I especially enjoyed hearing about the new dispatch hub, and your question about shift handovers stayed with me: that is exactly the kind of problem I enjoy solving. Happy to share anything else you need. Ananya

The same day, while memory is fresh, write down every question you were asked and how you answered. Questions repeat across companies more than you would believe, and this log becomes your best preparation for the next interview. If no timeline was given, one polite follow up after about a week is appropriate.

Then keep going. An interview is not an offer, and even a strong interview usually leads to more interviews before anything is signed, so keep applying until you have accepted something in writing. When an offer does arrive, that is the moment to negotiate it, not before (how to negotiate your salary offer). And if you are rejected, ask for feedback; a specific reason from a real interviewer is worth more than a week of guessing.

Your interview week at a glance

  • Confirmed the format, the people and the time.
  • Researched the company in four layers and wrote real questions.
  • Mapped every must have in the job description to a story.
  • Built eight to ten STAR stories, each under two minutes.
  • Prepared for the technical, case or aptitude round specifically.
  • Practised out loud, recorded, and done one mock with interruptions.
  • Settled the logistics: tech, clothes, documents, route.
  • Prepared five or six questions, including the closer.
  • Sent a specific thank you within a day, and logged every question asked.
