How to Get the Most Out of GMAFJ

Habits and settings that lead to more applications, more replies and more interviews with GMAFJ.

GMAFJ finds jobs that match your profile, fills in the applications and submits them for you, one after another, at the pace you set. The app does the repetitive work. What turns those applications into interviews is a set of habits around it: a profile worth filling from, a focused search instead of a huge net, and a few minutes a day spent clearing questions and improving what the app has saved. Below is the whole routine, ending with a weekly schedule you can copy.

Start with a profile worth automating

Everything GMAFJ sends is assembled from your profile. The standard fields on every form, your name, contact details, education and experience, are copied straight from it. Written and judgement questions are answered with AI, using your profile and your job preferences as the source. Where the profile has nothing useful, the app can only give the answer you would most likely give, and that is never as good as the answer you would have written yourself.

The first habit is therefore the least glamorous: finish the profile before you chase volume. A handful of essentials are checked before your first run, your name, email, phone number with its country code, the countries you can work in, and whether you need visa sponsorship, and the app will not start a run until they are filled in. Everything beyond the essentials is where the quality comes from.

The written parts pull more weight than people expect. The app builds a clean PDF resume from your profile, or uses the resume you upload, and attaches it to every application. The same profile feeds the cover letters it writes whenever a role asks for one, and the answers it drafts to questions about your experience. A vague line in the profile becomes a vague line on every application; a specific one works everywhere at once. Write your experience bullets the way you would write a resume: what you did, on what, and what changed because you did it.

Two notes for different readers. If you are a student or a fresher, your education, projects, internships and campus responsibilities carry more of the application, so fill them fully. If you are experienced, notice period and current and expected pay come up often on Indian sites, while forms in the United States lean on work authorization and sponsorship, and your profile carries all of these. Improving the profile once also improves the profiles the app keeps filled for you on Naukri, Internshala, Glassdoor, ZipRecruiter, Monster, Foundit and Wellfound.

There is a second reason to care about the profile: everything the app answers is remembered, so a fact entered correctly once becomes the saved answer every time the same question appears again. For a walk through the profile section by section, see the guide to building your profile.

Choose focused titles and locations

Your job titles and locations decide what the app searches for, which makes them the difference between a useful run and a noisy one. A huge net does not just waste applications on jobs you would never take. It fills your saved answers with text written for jobs you do not want, and it can produce interviews you will end up declining.

Start with the two or three titles an employer would actually put in a posting. A fresher in Delhi looking for data work might choose Data Analyst and Business Analyst, not Analyst. If you are experienced, pick the level as well as the field: a senior person whose preferences include junior titles dilutes every run. Locations work the same way. List the cities you would genuinely work in, or remote if that is what you want, rather than a whole country as a safety net.

If you are searching for two very different kinds of role at once, remember that one profile can only describe one of you well. Pointing the app at one kind of role at a time keeps every application it sends coherent, and you can always change the titles and run again afterwards.

The results will tell you how you are doing. After a run or two, look at what was applied to, and if the titles or the places are drifting from what you want, tighten them. The app skips postings it has already applied to, so refining your preferences never creates duplicate applications. And the sites it can search are the ones you have signed into inside the app, so you choose exactly where each run goes looking.

The useful mix differs a little by market. In India, Naukri and Internshala are the natural picks, and Internshala is particularly good for internships and first roles. In the United States, ZipRecruiter covers cities in the United States and Canada, and Wellfound is strong for startup jobs. LinkedIn (Easy Apply), Indeed and Glassdoor work in both markets, and the app applies on company career pages too. When the titles, the places and the sites feel right, press Start applying.

Clear the checks every morning

Most applications go through on their own, from search to submitted. The ones that stop are the ones that should: a required question only you can answer, a consent to accept, an employer site that wants an account first, or a check such as an expired sign-in. None of these fail silently. They wait for you, in plain sight, on the Dashboard.

A question that needs you arrives as a card, the kind the app calls a Boost card. It shows the job title and company, the question itself as text you can read and copy, and a screenshot of just that section of the form. You get three choices: Skip, Let AI answer, or Answer manually. If you answer it yourself, the app brings the form forward with the question highlighted and everything else already filled in. The queue waits while the card is open, because the app sends one application at a time and your answer has to land in the form it belongs to.

When you answer yourself, the app asks one thing before it saves:

Will this answer be the same for other jobs?

Choose yes for anything true everywhere: your notice period, why you left your last job, how you describe your strongest project. Choose this job only when the honest answer depends on the role, such as why you want this particular one. Getting the scope right is what makes the next hundred applications faster, because a saved answer is reused the moment the same question appears again.

Other stops announce themselves in a banner above the Dashboard: a captcha to solve yourself in the app's browser, the app never solves one; a sign-in that has expired, fixed by signing in again in the same browser view; or a site asking for a slower pace. Each banner says what happened and what to do about it, and none of them takes more than a minute or two. An employer site that wants its own account does not stop everything either: the app does not create accounts, so it parks that posting and keeps the link for you. The Applications board backs all of this up with colour: a green outline means the application was sent, a yellow one means it is waiting on you.

The habit is small: open the Dashboard once a day and clear whatever is waiting. If you clear your checks every morning before work, the app has the whole day to work through the queue, and nothing sits frozen because a card was open and nobody looked. If you enjoy watching, the Now running card has a Watch live button that shows the run as it happens.

Improve the Answers library every week

Every answer the app gives on your behalf is saved: the question, the answer, whether it is reused for every job or bound to one, where it came from, what kind of field it fits, how many times it has been used and when it was last used. They all live in one place, the Answers library, where you can search, sort, edit and delete.

This is the highest leverage few minutes in the app. Sort by the times an answer has been used, read the top of the list, and put one question to each entry: would I say this out loud in an interview? If the answer is no, edit it. The improved version goes into every future application that asks the same question, so a single edit keeps paying. Delete outright anything that is wrong, and anything you would not stand behind.

Pay attention to the source. Answers you typed are yours and probably fine. Answers the AI drafted are the ones to read, especially in the first weeks, while the app is still learning how you talk. The app has a rule it does not bend: it never invents facts about you, no employers you did not have, no qualification you do not hold, and it never fills in passwords or government ID, tax or bank numbers. Questions that commit you to something, such as a consent to a background check, always come to you instead. Inside those boundaries, a draft can still be accurate and yet not sound like you, and that is exactly what the edit is for.

Done weekly, this makes every run a little better than the last, which is the whole idea. The guide to reviewing your answers shows the library in detail, column by column.

Save Cold Mail for the jobs that matter most

Applications are forms. Cold Mail is the other door: a short, personal email from your own Gmail account to a recruiter or hiring manager at a company you want to work for. Because each message is researched, drafted and approved by you, it costs more attention than an application does, and that is exactly why it should not be spent everywhere. A short list aimed at the jobs you would be genuinely glad to take beats a long list aimed at anything that moves.

You choose the recipients, and you bring the addresses. The person who posted the job is the usual first pick, and the two details that give the research the most to work with are a work email address and a link to the person's profile. The app reads what that person says about themselves, drafts a message about them and the role, and shows it to you before anything is sent. Nothing goes out until you approve each draft, and edited drafts send as edited.

It is not a bulk mailer, by design. One message goes to one person, with no copies, and anyone who replies asking to hear nothing more is honoured for good. The habit worth building is a small weekly session: pick a handful of the week's jobs that you care about most, find who is behind them, add them, then approve or edit the drafts when you have ten quiet minutes. The guide to Cold Mail with GMAFJ covers the whole flow, from connecting Gmail to your first approved batch.

Pick a volume you can sustain

The app removes the cost of filling forms, so the cost of applying to more jobs is mostly the judgement around them: choosing titles, clearing checks, reading answers. That judgement is your time, and it is why volume needs a plan rather than an on switch. There is no daily limit in the app and the pace is yours to set. The useful question is not how many runs you can start but how many checks you can keep clear.

Consistency beats bursts. A run every weekday morning, with its cards cleared the next day, beats one enormous weekend run whose questions sit waiting until you are back at your desk, because a card left open is a queue not running. Whatever rhythm you choose, the weekly answer review and the cold mail session are the two appointments worth keeping.

How many applications is the right number depends on your market, your experience and how focused the search is, and the article on how many jobs to apply to works through that question properly. One rule holds either way: an application to a job you would not take is worse than no application at all, because the interview it wins is one you will have to withdraw from. Freshers can usually widen the net, more titles, more cities, internships included. Experienced people usually do better with a tighter search and a little more cold mail.

Keep going while you interview

Interviews take over your calendar, and the temptation is to stop applying until you hear back. Keep the search running. Offers arrive later than promised, first choices fall through, and a pipeline that stopped three weeks ago leaves you starting again from nothing. A short run each morning costs a few minutes of checks, and it does not care how busy your week has become.

The Applications board earns its keep here too. When a recruiter calls about a role, the board tells you what you applied to and where each application stands, so you never take the call blind. It is worth a glance before interviews as well: knowing what is still going out helps you give the honest answer when you are asked whether you are talking to other companies.

Once you have signed an offer, stop the runs and let everything go quiet. Reply to the recruiters you have been speaking with, and let the rest stand. The app will be there the next time you need it.

What an application tool cannot do

GMAFJ carries the whole application, but a few things stay yours, and it is worth being clear about them up front.

It cannot fix a thin profile. Where your profile has nothing useful, the app gives the answer you would most likely give, and it builds your resume from whatever you gave it. If the experience section is empty, no amount of runs will add substance to it. An hour spent on honest, specific bullets is worth more than a week of extra volume, and it is the one investment that improves every single application.

It cannot decide what you want. It applies to the titles and locations you choose, and it will not accept terms, create accounts, solve captchas, or answer questions that only you can answer. Those boundaries are not limitations to work around. They are what keep the applications honest and your accounts safe.

It cannot interview for you. Preparation, telling your story well, and negotiating an offer are skills of their own, and the application is only what gets you into the room. Spend some of the time the app gives back on those.

A weekly routine you can copy

None of this needs more than a few minutes a day. Here is the whole habit as a routine.

Every morning, about five minutes

  • Open the Dashboard and clear any cards waiting on you, choosing the right scope so each answer is reused wherever it honestly can be.
  • If a banner is pausing the run, a captcha, an expired sign-in or a request to slow down, deal with it and let the queue continue.
  • Glance at the Applications board: green for sent, yellow for waiting on you, and note anything a recruiter might call about.

Two or three times a week

  • Check your titles and locations still describe what you want, and tighten or widen them based on what the last runs produced.
  • Start a run, and watch the first application live if you want to see how it goes.

Once a week, about half an hour

  • Sort the Answers library by times used, and edit or delete anything you would not say in an interview.
  • Read the reasons shown on skipped and parked postings and act on what they say. An employer site that wants its own account keeps its link, so you can open it and apply there yourself.
  • Add a short list of Cold Mail recipients for the jobs you care about most, then approve or edit the drafts.

Once a month

  • Add anything new to the profile, a project shipped, a certification finished, a responsibility taken on, so the next application carries it.

That is the whole routine. The app does the filling, the submitting and the remembering, and it gets better at all three the longer you use it. Your part is the judgement: the profile, the preferences, the answers and the occasional approval. It is the smaller half of the work, and it is the half that turns a pile of applications into interviews.
