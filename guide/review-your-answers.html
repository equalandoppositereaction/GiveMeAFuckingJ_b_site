How GMAFJ Answers Application Questions, and How to Review Them

Where every answer comes from, how to check and edit your saved answers, and how to make the next ones better.

Every application GMAFJ sends is assembled from answers: your name and phone number, your notice period, whether you need visa sponsorship, and what you would tell an employer that asks why you want to work there. This guide covers where those answers come from, which ones you never need to think about, and which ones deserve a few minutes of your attention. It then walks through the Answers library, where every saved answer lives, and shows how reviewing and improving them makes each future application a little better than the last.

Where every answer comes from

Answers come from three sources, tried in a fixed order. The app uses the first that fits, and the Answers library tells you which source produced every answer it saved.

Your profile first. Most fields on any application are filled by copying straight from the profile you keep in the app: contact details, education, experience, work eligibility, notice period, and your current and expected pay. Nothing is decided at this stage, which is what you want for facts. It is also why the profile you build matters more than anything else in the app: a fact entered there correctly is answered correctly everywhere, and stays that way until you change it.

Pay shows how one fact covers many wordings. Sites in India ask for your current CTC and expected CTC; sites in the United States ask for your current and expected salary or compensation. The app treats these as the same profile detail wearing different words, so you enter the number once and it matches whichever phrasing a form throws at it.

Saved answers second. Every answer given on your behalf, whether you typed it or the AI drafted it, is saved against the question it answered. The next time any employer asks the same question, the saved answer goes in at no cost and no delay. The app is careful about the matching: a saved answer is reused when the question is the same or a genuine rewording of it, in the same kind of field with the same choices. So an answer written for a text box is never forced into a drop-down, and a question about your years of experience with one tool is never treated as the same question as another tool. Your five years of SQL cannot quietly become five years of Java.

A fresh AI draft last. For a question your profile cannot answer and nothing saved matches, the app drafts an answer with AI, using your profile and job preferences as its source. Where your profile has no answer, it gives the answer you would most likely give. The draft is saved like any other, so a question costs a draft once and is reused after that. Cover letters are the one deliberate exception: the app writes a fresh one for each job that asks, tailored to that role, rather than keeping a reusable one.

The honesty rule: the app does not invent facts about you

GMAFJ has one rule it does not bend: it does not invent facts about you. The AI is not allowed to produce a qualification, an employer, a certification or a licence you do not have, and it will not produce a phone number, email address or postal address that is not yours. Where it guesses, it guesses the kind of thing you could honestly say, never a new fact about your life.

The same rule decides what comes to you instead of the AI. Questions that only you can answer are kept away from it entirely: consent questions such as whether you agree to a background check, and anything that would sign you up for a service or commit you to money. The app never types passwords, and it never fills in government ID, tax, bank or card numbers.

It also refuses to guess when an answer does not fit. If a form offers a drop-down of ranges and the answer in hand does not clearly land in one of them, the app stops and asks you rather than picking the nearest option. And a question about a named country, such as whether you are legally authorized to work in the United States, is answered from the countries you listed as ones you can work in, never from a general yes that sounds good.

Which questions the app answers for you, and which come to you

The app does not wait for you to approve every answer. Applications can be submitted without you reviewing each one, which is exactly why it matters which questions the app decides on its own and which it will not. The split is simpler than you might expect.

Answered without you

  • Facts from your profile: contact details, education, experience dates, work eligibility, notice period and pay.
  • The voluntary reporting questions, gender and veteran status among them, answered once from your profile, with Prefer not to say on offer for every one of them.
  • Questions you have answered before, on this application or any other, and their genuine rewordings.
  • Screening questions the profile can work out, such as your total years of experience and the years per skill you listed in the years-per-skill section.
  • Drop-downs, multiple choice and yes-or-no questions, where the app picks from the options the form actually offers. Yes and no questions get a yes or a no, not a sentence.

Brought to you on a card

When the app has no answer it trusts, the question comes to you on a card at the top of the dashboard, which the app calls a Boost card. The card shows the job's title and company, the question itself, and a crop of the form so you can see the question in place. The queue waits while it is open: nothing goes out until you answer it or skip the job.

You get three choices: answer it yourself, let the AI answer, or skip this job. If you would rather answer inside the form, the app hands you the browser with the question highlighted and carries on once you are done. You are also asked whether to remember the answer for every job that asks the same question, or for this job only. That choice is worth a moment, and the scope section below explains why.

Two more things help you keep an eye on all of this. While a run is filling forms you can watch it live, and the applications board uses colour to show where things stand: a green outline means the application was sent, a yellow one means it is waiting on you to answer something. If the AI cannot be reached at all, the card says so rather than pretending a question was answered. The guide to when a job needs you walks through the card flow in detail.

The Answers library

Every saved answer lives in one place, the Answers library. Open it in the app and you get a table you can search, sort, edit and delete from: one row per answer, with columns for the question, the answer, the scope, the field type, the source, how many times the answer has been used, and when it was last used.

Four of those columns carry the meaning.

Source: profile, user or AI

Every answer shows where it came from. Profile means it was copied from your profile. User means you typed it, on a card or by editing. AI means the app drafted it. Filter to the AI answers first when you review, because they are the only ones you did not write yourself.

Scope: every job or this job only

Scope separates answers the app may reuse anywhere from answers that belong to one application. "How many years of SQL experience do you have" is good for every job. "Why do you want to work at Acme" is good for exactly one. The app handles the second case on its own: it recognises that questions naming a company are the same question everywhere, and it keeps the answer attached to the job it was written for rather than reusing one company's answer at another. The scope decisions that can go wrong are the ones the app leaves to you on a card, and the library is where you correct them, because editing an answer and changing its scope are both ordinary actions here.

Field type

This is the kind of control the answer belongs to: a text box, a drop-down, a yes-or-no, a date. It matters because answers are only reused into the same kind of control, and a drop-down answer only where the offered choices match. It is one more guard against a plausible-looking answer landing in a field it was never written for.

Times used

This column tells you where an edit pays out. An answer that has gone out on dozens of applications deserves more care than one used twice. It also shows which answers are doing the most work for you, which is a good way to decide what to sharpen first.

Above the table sit filter chips: AI-answered, Used 10+ times, and This job only. And if you are looking for one specific question, the search bar at the top of the app has a shortcut: type a, then a space, and it searches your saved answers by question text.

Reviewing your saved answers

Reviewing is not a gate the app forces you through; it is a habit that keeps quality climbing. Your first run saves a batch of AI drafts you have never read. Ten minutes in the library turns them into answers you would put your name to, and every application after that reuses your versions.

A rhythm that works:

  1. After your first run, open the library and filter to AI-answered. Read each answer against its question. These drafts were written without ever meeting you, and you are the only check on whether they sound like you.
  2. After your next few runs, look again. New questions will have been drafted and new answers saved. Once the same questions start coming back already answered, this part of the habit can relax.
  3. Whenever your profile changes, search the library for answers that touch what changed. A new job, a finished certification, a new expected salary or a changed notice period all make old answers stale.
  4. Now and then, sort or filter by use and read the top of the list. Those are your most repeated sentences, and they deserve to be your best.

What to fix when you read one

  • Stale facts. An old salary, an old notice period, a tool you no longer use. Edit the answer to match your profile, or delete it and let the app answer fresh next time.
  • Generic wording. "Passionate professional with strong communication skills" could describe anyone, so it convinces no one. Replace it with something only you could have written.
  • Wrong scope. A company-specific answer marked for every job is the worst kind of mistake, because it reads as carelessness to the one employer it was never meant for. A general answer you keep retyping has the opposite problem, and should be marked for every job.
  • Wording that is not yours. The AI writes plainly, but if a draft does not sound like you, rewrite it in your own voice. It will be reused word for word, so make it yours.

Deleting is a first-class action in the library, not a loss. An answer that is no longer true will be reused just as faithfully as one that is, so delete without ceremony.

Before and after: two answers, improved

Two worked examples. Both start from the kind of answer that technically fits the question and does nothing with it.

Example 1: why do you want to work here

This question is always kept to this job only, because an honest answer names one company. A first draft, written from a thin profile:

I want to work at Acme because it is a market leader with a great culture and exciting projects. I believe my skills and experience would make me a strong fit for your team.

Nothing in that is false, and nothing in it is true either. It could be sent to any company, which is why it convinces none of them. Here is the same answer after an edit that adds what only you know:

I have used Acme's reporting dashboard nearly every week for two years in my current role, so I know the product from the user's side. This role is about rebuilding the data pipeline behind that dashboard, which is the work I have been doing this year with our in-house reporting tool.

Every specific in the second answer is yours, which is exactly why the app could not write it alone. Your profile says what you have done; only you know that you used Acme's dashboard every week. When a card brings this question for a job you actually want, that is the material to reach for: one true, checkable detail is worth more than any amount of enthusiasm.

Example 2: describe your relevant experience

This one is reusable, so an edit here pays out on every future application that asks something like it.

I am a hardworking and dedicated professional with strong analytical skills and a passion for learning new technologies.

After the edit:

Three years turning messy retail data into reports people actually use, in SQL and Excel. Most recently I rebuilt a weekly sales report that twelve store managers rely on, and automated the data pull behind it in Python.

Notice what changed and what did not. Every fact comes from the same profile: the tools, the years, the report, the managers. The edit adds only shape: a first sentence that answers the question asked, followed by proof. That is the division of labour in one paragraph. The app holds your facts honestly, and your edit decides which of them earn a place and in what order. For the principles behind a strong written answer, the tips article on answering application questions goes deeper.

How better answers make the next applications better

The review loop is what turns the app from a form filler into something that improves with use. It works because of how the answer store behaves.

  • Answers are reused word for word. Fix a sentence once and it is fixed on every future application that asks the same question. You never have to fix it again.
  • Similar questions find the same answer. Employers word the same question a dozen different ways. Once a good answer is saved, the rewordings pick it up, as long as the field type and the choices genuinely match.
  • Profile fixes flow through. Many answers are copies of profile fields. Correct the profile and those correct themselves everywhere at once, with no editing at all.

The effect compounds. Your first run is the roughest, because the app has the fewest saved answers and has never seen you edit one. Each review session improves the answers that will be reused most, and each card you answer well is a question no employer can ever ask you again. Run, review, edit, run again: after a few cycles, the applications going out are written almost entirely in sentences you chose.

It cuts the other way too. An answer you never look at is reused just as obediently as one you polished. That is the whole argument for the habit: the app's memory is permanent, so pointing it at good answers is the highest-leverage few minutes in the app. The guide to getting the most out of GMAFJ collects the rest of the habits worth building.

A review checklist

The short version, for pinning somewhere.

  • Open the Answers library after your first run and read every AI-answered answer once.
  • Fix or delete anything stale, generic or untrue the day you notice it.
  • Check that company-specific answers are marked this job only, and general ones for every job.
  • Filter to Used 10+ times now and then, and put your care where the reuse is.
  • Search the library after any profile change and update the answers that touch what changed.
  • Fill in the years-per-skill section of your profile, so the most common screening questions answer themselves.
  • When a card comes to you, write the answer you would be happy to send many times over, and choose the scope deliberately.

Do that and the honesty rule starts working in your favour: every fact the app sends is yours, checked by you, in wording you chose. The applications go out on their own. The quality stays in your hands.
