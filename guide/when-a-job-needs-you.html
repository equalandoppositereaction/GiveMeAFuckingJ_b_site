What to Do When a Job Needs You

Questions only you can answer, terms to accept, sign-ups and checks: how GMAFJ asks for your help, and how to give it.

Most applications GMAFJ sends go through on their own: the app finds the posting, fills in the form from your profile, attaches your resume and submits. Now and then a form asks something the app will not decide for you, and the run stops right there instead of guessing. This guide shows you every kind of stop, how to clear each one, and how to keep the stops short and rare.

What a job can ask of you

An application form is someone else's machinery, and no two are built alike. Most questions they ask, the app answers by itself: first from your profile, then from answers it saved on earlier forms. What reaches you is the leftover, and it falls into four kinds.

  • Questions only you can answer. A required question that neither your profile nor your saved answers can supply. The app shows it to you on a card and waits.
  • Terms and consents to accept. Agreements, privacy policies, account rules. Accepting something is a decision only you can make, so the app leaves it, and the tick box that goes with it, to you.
  • Sign ups and account gates. Some employers take applications only on their own site, and that site may want an account first. The app does not create accounts, so it parks the posting and keeps the link for you.
  • Other checks. A captcha, an expired sign-in, a site asking the app to slow down. These pause the whole run, because they are about your account rather than about one application.

Two boundaries are worth knowing up front. Only required questions stop a run: if a form has an optional field the app cannot answer, it skips that field silently and moves on, and you never hear about it. And the app fills gaps where a gap is an honest answer: a required box about a record your profile does not hold, such as an old employer or a leave date, gets N/A on an application rather than stopping anything.

What the app will not do is answer a question where a wrong answer is worse than a pause. Work eligibility is the sharpest example. A question that names a country, such as whether you are legally authorized to work in the United States, is a question about that country. If your profile records authorization in India, the app will not answer yes on your behalf; it stops and asks you. The general form of the question, with no country named, it answers from your profile.

Questions that might otherwise come to you usually fill themselves. Notice period and current salary come up often on Indian job sites, and your profile holds both. United States forms lean on authorization and sponsorship instead, and your profile carries those too. What is left after all of that is genuinely yours, and that is what a card is for.

How the app asks for help

When a run reaches one of these stops, it does not fail quietly and it does not press on regardless. The application freezes exactly where it stands, and GMAFJ puts the reason in front of you.

Waiting questions appear as cards at the top of the Dashboard, above everything else, because the queue is blocked until they are answered. A card shows a screenshot of just the section of the form that is asking, the job title and the company, and the question itself as real text you can read and copy. Beneath the card are three actions: Skip, Let AI answer, and Answer manually.

The other checks announce themselves differently. A captcha, an expired sign-in, or a site demanding a slower pace pauses the entire queue, and a full-width banner appears above the Dashboard saying what happened and what to do about it. The Applications board, the list of jobs you have applied to, carries the same information quietly: a green outline means the application was sent, and a yellow outline means it is frozen on a question and waiting on you.

You can also watch. The Now running card on the Dashboard has a Watch live button that shows you the run as it happens, in the app's own browser view. While you watch, a strip above the page says what the engine is doing, and when it stops on a question, that strip is where you answer it.

One behavior underlies all of this: the app sends one application at a time, and while a card is open, nothing else in the queue starts. That is deliberate. Your answer has to land in the form it was meant for, and a queue running behind an open card could land it in a form that has since moved on.

If you want the wider picture of what a run does from search to submitted application, the guide on applying through job sites covers the whole path.

Questions only you can answer

The card with a question on it is called a Boost card, and it is the most common kind of help a job will ask of you. It appears when a form reaches a required question that nothing on file can fill: how you heard about the company, why you want this particular role, something about a project only you can describe, or a field the site itself refused. In that last case, the card names the field the site complained about, so you know exactly what to fix.

Answering a Boost card, step by step

  1. Open the Dashboard. The card sits at the top, with the question, the job, and a screenshot of that section of the form.
  2. Press Answer manually. The app brings its browser view forward, scrolls to the question, and draws a highlight ring around it. Everything else on the form is already filled in.
  3. Type your answer, or pick from the options. Take whatever time you need; the run is not going anywhere.
  4. Press the done button on the strip above the page, the one that says you are finished. A Skip this job button sits beside it if you would rather drop the application.
  5. The app reads the field back before it believes you. If the field is still blank, it says so, plainly, and keeps waiting. Nothing is assumed and nothing is guessed.
  6. Answer the reuse question described below, and the run resumes exactly where it stopped.
Answer the highlighted question, then continue.

A detail worth knowing: after you continue, the app re-reads the whole step instead of trusting the form to be as it left it. Forms grow new fields in response to earlier answers, and some sites wipe a box the moment you look away, so the app checks rather than hopes. If the site's own validation refused the form earlier, for example over a phone number it did not like, the card tells you which field drew the complaint.

Should the answer be reused?

Once your answer reads back cleanly, the app asks one question: "Will this answer be the same for other jobs?" Answering yes stores it for every future posting that asks the same thing. Answering no keeps it bound to this job only.

Neither choice is always right. Why you want to work at a particular company is a this-job-only answer. How you heard about a company might honestly be the same every time. A salary you would accept depends on the role, so treat those with care. The habit that pays off: mark an answer reusable when it truly describes you, and the next form that asks it will fill itself without stopping.

Changed your mind later? The Answers library holds every saved answer with its scope, where it came from, and how often it has been used. You can edit an answer, delete it, or flip it between reusable and this-job-only at any time, and the app's search bar finds saved answers by the text of the question.

When skipping is the right call

Skip abandons that one application, records why, and moves the queue on. The job is not counted as sent, and skipped postings do not appear on the Applications board. The app keeps the reasons and shows them grouped, so you can see at a glance why postings were passed over. If the same reason keeps appearing, that is a profile gap you can close once rather than a question you must keep answering by hand.

If you would rather not type at all, Let AI answer drafts an answer for that one field, using your profile and the job. Whatever it drafts is saved like any other answer, which means you can review it afterwards in the Answers library and correct anything you would not send.

Terms, consents and agreements

Some steps do not ask a question so much as demand an agreement: the site's terms of use, a privacy policy, an account agreement, a consent box the form refuses to continue without. The app treats these differently from ordinary questions, because agreeing on your behalf is not a decision software should make.

So the rule is simple: the app does not accept agreements for you. Where a form insists on a required consent that nothing on file can supply, the app stops at that step and shows it to you as a card, exactly like any other question it refuses to guess at. You accept it yourself on the real form, with everything around it already filled in, and the run carries on.

The protection also runs the other way. Forms love to pre-tick boxes you never asked for. LinkedIn's review step, to take a real example, carries a follow-the-company box already ticked, where leaving it set would quietly change your own account. GMAFJ clears pre-ticked marketing boxes like that before an application goes out. It never ticks anything of its own, and it leaves the boxes a site marks as required consents exactly as the site set them.

Not every ceremony needs you. Pages that carry nothing but a statement and a Next button, such as an equal-opportunity statement at the start of a form, are paged through by the app itself until real fields appear. A required group of tick boxes left empty gets its own None of the above option where the form offers one. The app bothers you only when a genuine decision is involved.

Sign ups and account gates

Plenty of employers do not take applications through the big boards. They send you to their own career page, often built on a hiring platform such as Greenhouse, Lever or Ashby, and usually the app applies there just as it does anywhere else. Sometimes, though, the first thing the page wants is an account: an email box and a Continue button that starts a sign-up rather than an application.

When that happens, the app neither pushes through it nor skips it. It parks the posting as an account gate and keeps the link, so nothing is lost and nothing was signed up without you. Opening the account is yours to do, and for good reason: the app never types a password, and most employers want to verify your email address from your inbox anyway.

Clearing a gate takes a couple of minutes:

  1. Open the link the app kept for you. It leads to the employer's own page, exactly where the run stopped.
  2. Create the account: your email, a password of your choosing, and the verification message if one arrives.
  3. Sign in on the employer's page and send the application again. The account was the only thing in its way.

A related sight on some career pages: a shortcut offering to apply using an account you hold with a job board. The app passes those over and uses the page's own application form instead, which keeps your applications where you can see them and your accounts doing only what you asked of them.

The same principle governs the job sites themselves. GMAFJ works through your own signed-in sessions in its built-in browser, and you sign in yourself; the app never sees your passwords. If a board has never been signed in, or has logged you out on its own, the guide to signing in to job sites walks through it step by step.

Checks that pause the run

The last group of stops is not about one form at all. These are the site protecting itself, or your account needing you, and they pause the whole queue behind a banner above the Dashboard, because nothing else can proceed until they are cleared.

An expired sign-in

Sites expire sessions on their own schedules, and a session that worked yesterday may be gone today. When the app lands on a sign-in page mid-run, it stops and tells you to sign in again. Open the browser tab, sign in as yourself, and let the run resume. The app is deliberately strict about what counts as signed in: a session is called good only when the site has actually served results through it, so the app will not tell you everything is fine while the site disagrees.

A captcha

If a site puts a captcha in the way, the app pauses everything and shows a card: "Solve the challenge in the browser, then resume." GMAFJ never attempts to solve or bypass a captcha. The challenge exists so the site can decide who is real, and the person it is asking about is you. Solve it, resume, and the queue picks up where it left off.

A site asking for a slower pace

Job boards sometimes answer a burst of activity by refusing requests for a while. The app paces its own activity to protect your accounts, but a site can still demand a longer pause. When it does, the app stops the queue, names the site and suggests a wait, and resumes only when you say so. Take the suggested wait. Pushing through a site's slowdown is how a recoverable pause turns into a restricted account, and no single application is worth that.

Two smaller checks resolve without you. A posting whose form the app genuinely cannot read fails on its own, the reason is recorded, and the queue carries on; if it keeps happening, the app pauses and waits for you rather than working through the rest of the queue blindly. A network hiccup is retried before the posting is given up on. Neither of these is a question for you, and neither blocks anything but its own application.

Numbers the app will never type

One rule has no exceptions anywhere in the app: it never types a password, and it never fills in an ID number, a tax number, a bank account or a card number, whatever the form asks. If a posting demands those, the fields are yours to complete by hand, and the app stops and shows you the question instead of filling it. A form that wants your bank or card details just to take your application deserves scrutiny for another reason too: think before you give them.

What happens if you ignore a card

Nothing dramatic, which is the point. The run waits. The application with the open card does not go out, and the rest of the queue does not start either, because the app applies to one job at a time. On the Applications board, the job sits with a yellow outline: frozen on a question and waiting on you.

The wait has no deadline. The app will not time a card out and fill it by guessing; the whole reason it stopped is that the answer must come from you. Close the app, come back tomorrow, and the card is still there, rebuilt exactly as it was, screenshot included. When you resume, the app re-opens the posting and re-fills the form from your saved answers, including the answer you gave if you had already given one. Your answers are the durable part; the browser session is not.

So the cost of ignoring a card is not lost work. It is lost time: a queue that would have spent the afternoon applying for you sits still behind one unanswered question, and every job behind it waits its turn.

A once-a-day habit that keeps runs moving

You do not need to watch the app work. You do need to give it a few deliberate minutes a day, and it works best if you do it the same way each time.

  1. Clear the cards. Open the Dashboard and answer whatever waits at the top, oldest first. Mark an answer reusable when it is, and that question never stops a run again.
  2. Scan the board. Look down the Applications board for yellow outlines. Each one is an application frozen on a question. Clear them the same way you clear cards.
  3. Read the skips. Check why postings were passed over. The reasons are grouped, so one glance tells you whether the same thing keeps happening; if it does, close the gap in your profile once instead of answering the question a dozen times.
  4. Audit a few answers. The Answers library holds everything the app has saved, its own drafts included. A wrong answer gets reused on every future application until you fix it, so edit or delete anything you would not send today. Reviewing your answers covers this in full.
  5. Top up your sign-ins. Before a longer run, sign in to the sites you want it to use. A run that starts on good sessions has one fewer reason to stop.

The habit shrinks the checks as well as clearing them. Cards mostly appear because a form asked something new; every question you answer as reusable is one the next posting will not ask again. And before a run even starts, the app checks that your profile and resume hold what applications require, and refuses to begin if something is missing, naming the fields. A complete profile quietly prevents a whole class of stalls.

Your checklist

  • A card at the top of the Dashboard means a required question is waiting. Answer it, skip it, or let the app draft it.
  • A yellow outline on the Applications board means frozen on a question and waiting on you. A green outline means sent.
  • A banner above the Dashboard means a check paused the whole queue: sign in again, solve the captcha yourself, or give the site the wait it asked for.
  • Agreements and consents are always yours to accept. Pre-ticked marketing boxes are cleared for you.
  • An account gate means the employer wants an account first. Create it from the link the app kept, then send the application again.
  • The app never types passwords, or ID, tax, bank or card numbers. Those fields are yours.
  • An ignored card waits indefinitely, and the queue waits with it. A few minutes a day keeps the applications going out.
