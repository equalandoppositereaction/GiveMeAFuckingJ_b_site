Your Resume in GMAFJ: Upload One or Build One

How GMAFJ uses your resume, how to upload your own, and how to build a clean PDF resume from your profile.

Every application GMAFJ submits carries your resume, so that file is worth ten minutes of care. The app gives you two routes: it can build a clean, professional PDF resume from the profile you keep in the app, or it can use a resume you upload. Both routes are free, both take minutes, and you can redo either at any time. This guide explains how each route works, which one fits your situation, and how to keep your resume and your profile telling the same story.

How GMAFJ uses your resume

Every application needs a resume attached, which is why the app asks you to sort yours out during setup rather than in the middle of a run. Once you have chosen a file, the app does the rest: it opens each application form, fills in the fields, attaches your resume, writes a cover letter if the form asks for one, and submits, all from your own signed-in account on the job site.

The file itself is never summarised, rewritten or reformatted. Whatever you chose as your resume, that exact file is what the form receives. If the app built a PDF from your profile, the built PDF goes out. If you uploaded your own file, your file goes out exactly as you saved it.

Your profile is doing double duty here, which is the part people miss. The app answers form questions from your profile, and the built resume comes from the same profile, so a fact you correct in one place corrects everywhere. It is also why a complete profile matters even if you upload your own resume: the resume gets you read, but the profile fills the form. For a section-by-section tour of what the profile holds, read how to build a complete profile.

Upload your own resume

If you already have a resume you are happy with, upload it. The app accepts PDF and DOCX files and keeps the file on your own computer. You can upload it while you are filling in your profile and work from that, typing in the parts your resume does not cover, because the two routes are not exclusive.

What the app does with your file

Nothing, and that is the point. It does not edit your wording, swap your fonts, or rebuild your layout. The stored file is attached unchanged to every application, so what a recruiter opens is precisely what you saved. Treat the upload as handing the app a stack of printed copies: it hands them out for you, it does not rewrite them.

One check happens at each form. Application forms state what kinds of file they accept, and the app respects that: if a form only accepts PDF and your resume is a DOCX, the app will not force the upload and hand the site something it would bounce. That application does not go through, which is the app failing safe rather than submitting a broken file. When in doubt about what to upload, a PDF is the safe choice.

Choosing a file that travels well

If your resume is a few years old, open it once before uploading and check the basics. It should be a text PDF rather than a scan or a photograph, because a picture of text cannot be read by the software that screens resumes. Your contact details should be real text, not part of a header image. Name the file something a human can identify at a glance, in the shape firstname-lastname-resume.

If the file fails any of these checks, rebuilding it is usually quicker than patching it. The next section shows a way to get a clean, current PDF with almost no effort, using work you have already done.

Build a resume from your profile

The second route needs no file at all: the app writes a resume for you from the profile you filled in. It uses one clean, professional template, so the layout is consistent every time you build, and the result is a proper PDF you can preview and save. Because the source is your profile, building a resume is really just a different view of work you have already done.

What goes into it

The built resume carries what your profile holds: your name and contact details, your links such as LinkedIn and a portfolio, your education including coursework if you added it, your experience with the bullets you wrote, your projects, your skills, and awards if you have any. The mapping is direct, which makes the resume easy to predict: the education part of your profile becomes the education part of the PDF, each job becomes an entry with its bullets under it, and each project appears with its description. If a section of the PDF looks thin, the fix is to add to that part of the profile, not to hunt for a setting.

There is a live preview, so you see the finished PDF before anything is saved. Beneath the Save button sits a small credit line to the template's original authors, which is how the app keeps its promise to the people who made the template.

Two details are worth knowing, because they are the things that usually go wrong with resume tools. First, characters: the ampersand in a company name, the percent sign in an achievement, the hash in C#, the underscore in a Python file name, the dollar sign in a salary, accented letters in names from any alphabet. All of these come out in the PDF exactly as you typed them. Second, text: the PDF is real text rather than a picture, so the programs that read and rank resumes can read yours.

Writing bullets worth sending

The built resume is only as good as the profile behind it, and the experience bullets are where the quality lives. A fair test is whether you could read a bullet out loud in an interview without embarrassment. Weak and strong look like this:

Before: Handled billing for the branch.
After: Ran billing for a 12-person branch, collected overdue payments worth about 8 lakh rupees a year, and cut invoice errors by moving the team off spreadsheets and onto the company's billing system.

Freshers, the same standard applies to projects and internships, and you probably have more material than you think.

Built the registration site for a college hackathon that took 200 sign-ups over a weekend, added online payments when the waiting list grew past the hall's capacity, and fixed a double-booking bug an hour before doors opened.

If you want to sharpen the bullets themselves, read how to write a resume that gets interviews, then bring the improvements back into your profile so every future build picks them up.

One more thing the built route gives you: the PDF is put together on your own computer, offline. It is not sent anywhere until an application carries it to a job site, which is the same privacy posture as everything else in the app.

And if a build ever fails, you are not stuck. The app shows the technical detail of what went wrong instead of a vague message, offers to copy that detail so a support request is actually answerable, and can fall back to a plainer-looking version. The upload route is also always there: your own file is a permanent way around any problem with the builder.

Which route suits you

Match the route to your situation rather than to what sounds more impressive.

  • You are experienced and your resume is current. Upload it. The app sends your file untouched, so the formatting you invested in is preserved, including any deliberate design choices.
  • You have a resume but it is out of date. Build from the profile instead. Type your current facts in once, generate the PDF, and you have a clean, current resume in minutes. You can still update your own file later if you are attached to it.
  • You are a fresher with no resume at all. Build from the profile, and do not apologise for it. Coursework, projects and internships fill a first resume honestly, and a plain, clean one is all an application needs.
  • You want both. That works. Generate one and upload another, preview both, and keep whichever you prefer as the file that goes out.

Whichever you pick, filling in the profile is not optional, because the profile is where form answers come from. An uploaded resume does not fill in forms; the profile does. If you are still setting the app up from scratch, the getting started guide walks the whole path from install to your first run.

Keeping your resume and profile in step

Resumes go stale quietly. A title changes, a project ships, a tool gets learned, and the file on your computer still describes the person you were last year. The app cannot notice that for you, but it keeps the fix small if you work in the right order: profile first, then resume.

On the built route, the order is simple. Correct the profile, then rebuild the PDF, and the new version matches. You can rebuild at any time, not just during setup, and later applications pick up the newest saved version.

On the upload route, remember that the app never touches your file. The resume you uploaded in January is still the January version in June. When something meaningful changes, edit your master copy, export a fresh PDF, and upload it again. It is a two-minute habit, and it prevents the most embarrassing kind of mismatch, the one where the form says one thing and the attachment says another.

Moments that should trigger an update

  • A new job, a title change, or the end of a contract.
  • A project that shipped, especially one with a number attached to it.
  • A new skill or tool, including the years of experience per skill the app asks for separately.
  • An award, a certification, or a finished course.
  • Contact details, or your LinkedIn and portfolio links.
  • In India, your notice period, or your current and expected CTC.
  • In the United States, your work authorisation or sponsorship situation.

There is one more place stale facts hide. The app remembers the answers it gives on forms and reuses them, so an answer that was right in March can still be going out in September. The Answers library in the app lists every saved answer, searchable and editable, and it is worth a visit whenever something in your working life changes.

What recruiters and application forms actually receive

Follow one application through. The app opens the form on the job site, in your own logged-in session. It fills the fields from your profile and the answers it has saved. It attaches your chosen resume file. If the form asks for a cover letter, it writes one tailored to the role. Then it submits, and the employer receives an application that looks like one you sent yourself, because in every way that matters, you did.

The resume is the same file on every application. The app does not rewrite your resume for each job; the per-job variation happens in the form answers and the cover letter, not in the attachment. That makes the resume a fixed asset, which is an argument for making it good once and maintaining it, rather than hoping a tool will fix it later. To make the file itself genuinely hard to ignore, read the guide to getting past applicant tracking systems, which explains what the screening software looks for.

As for where the file travels: to the job site you are applying through, as part of the application you asked for, and nowhere else. The built PDF is assembled on your machine, your uploaded file stays on your machine, and the only time a resume leaves your computer is inside an application on its way to an employer.

A checklist before your first run

Run through this once and you are ready to start.

  • The profile basics are filled: your first and last name, your email, your phone number with its country code, the countries you can work in, and whether you need visa sponsorship. The app names anything still missing before a run starts.
  • Education, experience, projects and skills are filled in, with bullets you would be comfortable saying out loud.
  • Years of experience per skill are set, because forms ask this constantly and it cannot be guessed.
  • In India, your notice period and your current and expected CTC are real numbers rather than hopeful ones. In the United States, your work authorisation and sponsorship answers are correct.
  • Your resume is chosen: built from the profile, or uploaded as a PDF or DOCX.
  • If it is built, you previewed the PDF and read it end to end. If it is uploaded, you opened the file once and confirmed it is the current version, and a text PDF rather than a scan.
  • Nothing in the profile or the resume is a fact you would flinch to hear repeated back to you in an interview.

That is the whole job. Choose the route that fits, keep the profile ahead of the resume, and refresh both when your working life moves. After that, the resume takes care of itself.
