How to Build a Complete Profile in GMAFJ

What the profile holds, why each part matters, and how a complete profile means every application is filled in properly.

Your profile in GMAFJ is the one document every application is filled from. The app reads it to complete the standard fields on each form, draws on it to answer the written questions, and searches for jobs using the preferences you set. An hour spent making it complete and honest is repaid on every application, so this guide walks through it part by part: what each section holds, why it matters, and how to fill it well.

Why the profile matters this much

When the app fills in an application, most of what lands in the form comes straight from your profile: your name, contact details, work eligibility, education, experience and the rest. For questions that need a written or judgement answer, GMAFJ answers with AI, using your profile and job preferences as its source. Where the profile has nothing useful, the app can only give the answer you would most likely give, and that is never as good as the answer you would have written yourself.

Two other things run off the profile. The app can fill in your profiles on Naukri, Internshala, Glassdoor, ZipRecruiter, Monster, Foundit and Wellfound from the profile you keep in the app, so improving it once improves every one of those profiles too. And your job preferences decide which postings the app looks for in the first place, which makes them the difference between a useful run and a noisy one.

Every answer the app gives is remembered, so the same question never costs you time twice. That memory is only as good as its source, though. A profile with gaps does not stop the app from working; it just means some fields go out empty, or filled with the most likely answer rather than your answer.

A handful of fields are treated as essentials, because every posting asks for them: your name, your email, your phone number with its country code, the countries you can work in, and whether you need visa sponsorship. Before your first run the app lists any of these that are missing and explains why: without them an application stops at the first page. It will not start a run until they are filled in, so you cannot accidentally send out half an application.

If you have only just installed the app and are still finding your way around, the getting started guide covers setup from the beginning. This one goes deeper on the profile itself.

Identity, contact and links

Identity and contact

Identity is simple and worth doing properly. Your first and last name go on every application, so use the name that matches your official records, not a nickname. If you go by something else at work, add it as your preferred name, and add pronouns if you want them shown.

Contact holds your email, your phone number with its country code, and your address. The country code matters more than people expect, and it should be yours, not the job's: an application going to a company in Mumbai and one going to Chicago both need to reach you. If you are in India that is +91; in the United States, +1.

For the address, give what a resume would give: your city, state or region, postal code and country, with a street line if you are comfortable including one. Different forms ask for different levels of detail, and this section covers them all.

Links worth adding

The links section is short but pulls weight: LinkedIn, GitHub, a portfolio, and any other link with a label you choose. Add what a recruiter checking you out would want to find. LinkedIn is worth having for almost everyone; a GitHub or portfolio link matters most in software, design and writing; a student can link a class project site or a competition entry.

Label the extra links clearly. "Case studies" tells a reader what they will find; "link" tells them nothing. And open each link once in a browser while signed out, to check it shows what you think it shows.

Education, at each stage of your career

Each education entry holds an institution, a degree or course, a field of study, start and end dates, and two optional extras: your grades and a list of relevant coursework.

If you are a student or a fresher, education carries more of your application, so fill it fully. Use the coursework list for subjects the roles you want actually ask about; a future data analyst listing database and statistics coursework is answering a screening question before it is asked. Grades go in if they help you and stay out if they do not.

If you have years of experience, education can be brief: the qualification, the institution and the dates are enough. Leave grades empty rather than padding them. An empty field is rarely noticed, while an unflattering grade invites a question you would rather not answer.

Experience: bullets that do the work

Each experience entry holds a company, your title, an optional location, start and end dates with your current job marked as current, and a list of bullets. The fields take a minute. The bullets are the part worth an evening.

Write them the way you would write a resume, because that is close to where they end up: the app builds a PDF resume from your profile if you let it, and the written parts of your profile are what it draws on when a form asks about your experience. A good bullet says what you did, on what, and with what result. Compare "responsible for sales reporting" with:

Built and maintained the weekly sales report for 12 stores, cut preparation from two days to two hours by automating the data pull, and added a returns breakdown the merchandising team now uses every Monday.

One line, and it answers half a dozen questions a form might ask: what you did, at what scale, using what, to what effect. Two or three bullets like that per job is plenty.

Freshers and students: internships, part-time jobs, freelance work and campus responsibilities all belong here. Handling registrations for a college fest with 800 attendees is experience, and it is honest experience. Do not leave this section empty because you have not held a full-time job yet.

The principles behind strong bullets are the same as behind the resume itself, and the guide to writing a resume that gets interviews covers them in depth.

Projects and skills

Projects

A project holds a name, a short description, bullets and an optional link. This is the fresher's strongest section and the career switcher's second best, because it is proof of skill that does not depend on anyone hiring you first. Two or three real projects beat ten half-remembered ones.

A web app that splits shared household expenses: flatmates enter what they paid on their phones, the app totals it by month and works out who owes whom in the fewest transfers. Built with React and Node, used by about 200 students on my campus.

Notice what that description does. It says what the thing is, who uses it and what it is built with, in three lines. Add bullets underneath for the parts you are proud of: the bug that took two days to find, the decision you would defend in an interview. Link the live version if it exists.

Skills, and how long you have used them

Skills are stored in groups you name, so organise them the way a recruiter would read them: a group called Languages with SQL and Python in it, or one called Analytics with Excel and Power BI. Prune as well as add; a group containing every tool you have ever touched tells a reader nothing.

Alongside the skills themselves, the profile asks how many years of experience you have with each one, in a section of its own. Fill it in honestly and do not inflate it. Forms ask this constantly, usually as a drop-down of ranges, and the app cannot derive it for you: it can work out your total years from your job dates, but not how many of those years involved a particular tool. Five years of total experience might contain one year of SQL, and the honest one is the useful answer. Small numbers are fine, for freshers especially; a fresher listing SQL at one year still gets matched to the right range.

Work eligibility: the answers that decide where you can apply

This is the section that decides whether applications come out correct, and it is where India and the United States differ most. It is also the section where a wrong answer is repeated faithfully on every application until you fix it, so check it twice.

Countries you can work in should list every country where you are already allowed to work. The sponsorship answer is separate, and it must be true. Forms ask the question both ways, "will you now or in the future require sponsorship" and "are you able to work without sponsorship", and the app handles both phrasings, but only you can make the underlying answer correct. An honest yes costs you some listings; a dishonest no surfaces at the offer stage or the background check, which is far worse than a filtered application.

For India, the same section holds your notice period in days, your current CTC and your expected CTC, each with its own currency and a choice between a yearly and a monthly figure. Enter the notice period your contract actually specifies, not the one you hope to negotiate down from: if it is 60 days, put 60. If your notice is already being served, the earliest start date field is where the realistic day goes.

For the United States, the questions that matter are work authorisation and sponsorship, and usually an earliest start date rather than a notice period. There is also a willingness to relocate answer. Set it truthfully; saying you are open to relocation invites employers from cities you may not want, which is exactly the decision this field is there for you to make.

Freshers in India: your current CTC may be zero or a stipend figure, and that is fine. Enter what is true, and put a number you actually mean in the expected field rather than a token amount.

The voluntary reporting questions

The profile closes its personal sections with a set of voluntary reporting questions: gender, race, veteran status, disability status, and whether you are Hispanic or Latino. Every one is optional, and every one offers "Prefer not to say".

They are there so you answer each question once instead of typing it into every application that asks, and applications in the United States ask often. Answer what you are comfortable answering, and decline the rest with a clear conscience. Nothing else in the app depends on these answers.

Preferences: telling the app what to look for

Preferences are not about you; they are about the search. Job titles, locations, a choice between on-site, hybrid, remote or any of them, seniority, a minimum salary and companies to exclude together decide which postings the app applies to.

With titles, use the words employers advertise with rather than the one in your head, and add several variants. The person writing the listings does not know what you call your own job. Freshers should include the entry-level names of the role they want alongside the standard ones.

Locations should be the cities and regions you would genuinely accept, and the remote setting covers whether on-site, hybrid or remote suits you. There is a seniority choice too, which keeps the search aimed at the level you are actually at. The exclude list is for employers you never want to hear from again: places you have already interviewed with and been rejected by, or companies you have ruled out for your own reasons.

The minimum salary is the floor below which you would turn the job down, not the number you are hoping for. If you would not accept less than, say, 12 lakh a year in India or 90,000 dollars a year in the United States, that is the figure to enter. Set it honestly and the app quietly skips listings below it.

Start from your resume, then fix what it cannot know

You do not have to type all of this by hand. The profile offers two ways through: fill in the fields yourself, or upload a resume and let the app prefill the profile from it, then correct what it got wrong. Most people do both, and each section saves as you go, so a half-finished profile survives closing the app.

The upload is the faster start, but a resume is a poor source for several parts of a profile, so review these by hand every time. Your phone country code. Your work eligibility: countries, sponsorship, notice period, current and expected CTC. Your job preferences, which a resume never states. And the years of experience per skill, which no resume records in a form the app can rely on. Dates deserve a look too, because resumes are written for humans and dates are where they are messiest.

The uploaded file has a second job: it is what gets attached to your applications, in PDF or Word form, unless you prefer to have the app generate a clean PDF resume from the profile instead. Both routes, and how to choose between them, are covered in your resume in GMAFJ.

Keep the profile current

A profile is not a form you fill once; it is the source the app copies from. When you gain a skill, finish a project, change jobs or complete a certification, add it. The next application goes out containing the new fact, and nothing else needs doing.

Currency matters in the other direction too. The app remembers the answers it gives on your behalf, and you can review, edit or delete any of them in the Answers library, where each one shows the question, the answer, how often it has been used and where it came from. After a significant profile change, it is worth a look: an answer drafted from your old profile keeps being reused until you change it. The guide to reviewing your answers explains the library in full.

The same applies to your resume. If you use the generated one, it is built from the profile, so regenerate it after real changes. If you uploaded your own, upload the new version when your resume changes, so the attachment and the profile tell the same story.

The gaps that make applications weak

Most weak applications trace back to one of a handful of profile gaps. They are all quick to fix once you can see them.

  • No years per skill. The most common screening question on job sites is left to a guess. Fill the section in, honestly.
  • An ungrouped, unpruned skill list. Every tool you have ever touched, with no categories, tells a recruiter nothing. Group the real ones and cut the rest.
  • Duty bullets. "Responsible for testing" says what you were, not what you did. Rewrite each bullet as an action with a result.
  • Missing or dead links. A portfolio link that leads nowhere is worse than none. Check each one.
  • Stale work eligibility. A notice period from a job you have already left, a country list from before a visa came through, an expected CTC from two roles ago. Update it the day it changes.
  • Nothing in projects for a fresher. If your experience section is thin and your projects section is empty, the application has nothing to talk about. Write up two things you have built.
  • Preferences copied from a wishlist. One exact title and one city will find few postings; fifteen loosely related titles will apply to jobs you do not want. Aim for a sensible middle, and refine after your first run.
  • An old phone country code or an email you no longer check. The app fills these in exactly as your profile says, which is the problem when the profile is wrong.

A checklist before your first run

Run through this once before you start applying, and again whenever something about your situation changes.

  • Name and contact details filled in, with the right phone country code.
  • Links added, and each one opened once to check it works.
  • Education complete, with coursework listed if you are a student or fresher.
  • Every job entry has at least one bullet that says what you did and what it achieved.
  • Two or three projects described, especially if you are a fresher or switching fields.
  • Skills grouped, with honest years of experience per skill.
  • Countries you can work in, and the sponsorship answer, checked twice.
  • For India: notice period, current CTC and expected CTC entered truthfully. For the United States: authorisation, sponsorship and earliest start date.
  • Voluntary reporting questions answered or declined, whichever you prefer.
  • Titles, locations, remote setting, seniority, minimum salary and excluded companies set.
  • Resume chosen: uploaded or generated from the profile, with the preview checked.

Fill the profile once, properly, and every application after it starts from your best foot already forward. Then keep it alive: five minutes of updating when something changes, and the app keeps applying with the truth.
