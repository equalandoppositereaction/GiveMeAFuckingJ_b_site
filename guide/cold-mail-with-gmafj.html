How to Email Recruiters With Cold Mail

Connecting your Gmail, choosing whom to write to, reviewing each draft and sending personal emails from your own account.

Applications are forms. Cold Mail is the other door: a short, personal email, written in your own name, from your own Gmail account, to a recruiter or hiring manager at a company you want to work for. GMAFJ helps you choose the recipients, drafts each message, and sends nothing until you have read it and clicked Send. This guide walks through the whole thing, from connecting Gmail to your first approved batch.

What Cold Mail is for

Cold Mail sends personal emails, one person at a time, from your own account. It exists because an application form puts you in a queue, and a two-paragraph email from a real person can put a name to that queue position. The two work together: you apply through the job sites, and you write to the humans behind the jobs you care about most.

It is not a bulk mailer. Every message goes to exactly one recipient, with no BCC lists and no copies. That is deliberate, because a message addressed to thirty people at once is not personal, and it is exactly the pattern spam filters are built to catch.

You will find everything on the Cold Mail page in the app: your recipients, the drafts being written, and the approval queue where you decide what goes out. The Dashboard keeps a count of your cold mails sent alongside your applications, with a this-week figure beside it.

Connecting your Gmail

Cold Mail sends from your Gmail account, through Gmail itself. The first time you use it, you sign in to Gmail inside the app, in the same browser view you use to sign in to job sites. There is no separate access request to grant: you are signing in as yourself, in your own session, and the app sends through the same Gmail you use every day.

Sometimes Google asks for extra proof that it is really you, such as a device verification or a security prompt. When that happens, the app pauses and you complete the step yourself, right there in the browser view. The app never fills in security checks and never tries to get around one.

On privacy, the promise is simple: your sign-ins to job sites and email stay on your computer, and GMAFJ never receives those passwords. Your cold mail history and your opt-out list stay on your computer too.

One practical tip: if you have more than one Google account, sign in with just the one you want to send from. The Mail tab shows the account the app last sent from, and the app re-checks the signed-in account each time it sends. Still, the cleanest setup is one account, the one your contacts should see in their inbox.

Choosing who to write to

The people worth writing to are recruiters and hiring managers on jobs you have found on the site or already applied to. You choose them and you bring their email addresses. Cold Mail does not look addresses up for you and does not scrape them from anywhere. That boundary is deliberate, and it is a big part of what keeps the mail personal and welcome.

You add recipients as a list, one per line, or paste them in. The email address is the only required part. Name, job title, company and a profile link are all optional, in whatever order you happen to have them:

dana@acme.com, Dana Real, VP Engineering, Acme, https://linkedin.com/in/danareal

The two details that change the draft

Two of the optional details do far more work than the rest, which is why the app points them out where you enter them:

  • A work email. A personal address carries nothing about the person. A work address carries the company, and often the person too, and that gives the research something real to work with.
  • A profile link. This one is worth the most. It tells the research exactly which person this is, instead of guessing from a name. A LinkedIn link works, and a Google Scholar profile works just as well, and better for anyone academic.

Without a name and company, or a link, there is not much to research. In that case the app tells you there was not enough public info rather than producing a generic draft, because a generic draft under your name is worse than no draft at all.

The app also tidies the list before anything else happens: duplicate addresses are removed, and anyone on your opt-out list is refused on the way in, before a single draft is written.

Where to find the addresses

Start with the job itself. Postings on LinkedIn, Indeed, Naukri and the other sites often name the person who posted them, and a company's own careers page sometimes does too. If you have already applied and a recruiter contacted you, their address is sitting in your inbox. Ten minutes of looking usually fills a short list, and a short list you actually chose beats a long one you did not.

How drafts get written

For each recipient, the app researches before it writes. When you have supplied a profile link, it opens that page in your own signed-in browser and reads what the person says about themselves: their headline, their about section, their recent activity, in their own words. The app reads the page and the AI writes the draft, which is how a draft ends up about the right person rather than about whoever happens to share their name.

That link matters more than it looks. Asked to write about a name alone, an AI will find the best-known person with that name, and that person is usually not the one you meant. Reading the actual page you pointed at avoids the namesake problem instead of hoping around it.

If the page cannot be read, for example because there is no link, the profile is private, or you are signed out of that site, the app falls back to public web information and tells you which path it took. If there is not enough public info either way, it skips that person instead of guessing.

The draft itself draws on three things: the research about the recipient, your profile, and the job you are writing about. Your profile is the part you control most directly, and a thin profile makes thin drafts. If yours is half empty, spend some time on it first; the guide to building your profile in GMAFJ covers what to fill in and why each field matters.

The drafts are written by AI. The site's AI Disclosure explains what that involves in full, and the short version is already in the FAQ: emails are never sent without your approval.

Every draft also has to pass the app's own checks before it reaches you. A draft without a usable subject line, for example, is marked as needing editing rather than being sent with an empty subject.

Attachments are off by default

Two toggles sit among the settings: Attach my resume and Attach a document. Both are off by default, on purpose. A resume attached to a first cold message is presumptuous when you are the one asking, and it is a common reason such mail gets filtered before it is read. The better move is to offer it in the reply.

If you do turn one on, know that the app takes it seriously. The resume toggle uses the same resume the app attaches to your applications, and if the file is missing when it is time to send, the send stops and the app names the file. A message that promises an attachment and arrives without one is a message you cannot fix afterwards.

Reviewing each draft

Nothing sends itself. Every draft waits in the approval queue and goes out only on an explicit click from you, one draft at a time. There is no approve-all button, no auto-send setting, and no way to schedule messages that skip review.

The approval card shows you four things: the recipient, the subject, the body, and the research bullets the draft was built from. The bullets are the part people skip and should not. If the research has picked up the wrong person or the wrong company, you will see it there, before the message goes out rather than after. Then you choose:

  • Send to approve this draft as it stands.
  • Edit to open the body inline and change it. Edited drafts send as edited, and the app still adds your signature and the opt-out line afterwards.
  • Skip to leave that person unsent. Skipping is a legitimate answer, and a queue where you skip half the drafts is working exactly as designed.

The reason for the gate is simple. These messages go to real people, under your real name and your real email address, and they land in inboxes that might interview you. A wrong fact in a cold email is a professional cost you cannot undo, so every draft gets your eyes before it gets anyone else's.

Sending from your own account

When you approve a draft, the app sends it through Gmail's own website. It fills in the compose window, the recipient, the subject and the body, presses Send, and then waits for Gmail's own confirmation that the message actually went. If that confirmation never appears, the send is recorded as failed and shown to you. It is never assumed.

Approved messages go out one at a time, spaced across the day rather than fired in a burst. The spacing is built in; you do not have to manage it.

Every message is built the same way: your text, then your signature, then one opt-out line at the bottom. The app appends those exactly once, even to a draft you edited, so you never need to paste in a sign-off yourself.

Gmail's own sending rules still apply to your account, whatever the app does. The app sets no quota of its own, because how many people you write to is not its decision to make. And if a send fails, or Gmail signals a problem, the whole batch stops. The drafts that did not go are shown to you, and nothing retries automatically behind your back. You decide what happens next.

Keeping your mail personal

The only real limit on Cold Mail is one you set yourself: a batch you will actually read. Every draft needs your click, so the queue moves at the speed of your attention. Five drafts you read closely beat fifty you skim, both for your evening and for the people receiving them.

A good draft is short and specific. It says why this person and not anyone else, names the job, makes one clear ask, and sounds like a person. Before approving one, run it against this list:

  • Does it name the actual job, and the actual reason you are writing to this person?
  • Is there one clear ask, such as a short call or a pointer to the right team?
  • Would you say this out loud to a stranger without wincing?
  • Is anything wrong in the research bullets, and if so, did you fix it with Edit?
  • If this is a first message, is it free of attachments?

Here is an example of the shape worth aiming for. It was written by hand for this article, not produced by the app, and the app would add the signature and the opt-out line below it:

Subject: Data analyst role on the growth team

Hi Dana,

I applied for the data analyst role on your growth team this week and wanted to introduce myself. I have spent the last two years building reporting in SQL and Python at a logistics company, and the self-serve dashboards your team wrote about are exactly the kind of problem I want to work on next.

If a short call would be useful, I am happy to work around your calendar. And if this is not your team, I would be grateful for a pointer to the right person.

Thank you for reading,

Priya

Notice what the example does not do. It does not list every skill, it does not attach a resume, and it does not pretend the recipient has been waiting for the message. It gives one reason, makes one ask, and respects the reader's time. If you want to go deeper on the writing itself, the tips article on cold emailing recruiters covers wording, follow-ups and timing.

The opt-out line, and staying welcome

Every Cold Mail message ends with the same line: "If you'd rather not hear from me, just reply 'no thanks' and I won't write again." It is plain, it is human, and the app honours it. When a reply asks to be left alone, the address goes onto a local opt-out list, the app will not write to it again, and the list is checked before drafting even starts. Someone who bowed out never sees a second message.

Messages are plain text: no tracking pixels, no link shorteners, no read receipts. Each of those hurts deliverability, and none of them belongs in a job seeker's email. Plain text also happens to read as what it is, a person writing to a person.

On the rules: one-to-one professional outreach from an individual looking for work is not bulk marketing, and the strictest parts of email laws, such as CAN-SPAM in the United States or the DPDP Act in India, are aimed at something else. The app still ships the careful version, because the cost is one line of text: your real identity, an honest subject line, and a working opt-out in every message.

When to use Cold Mail alongside applying

Apply first, then write. The application is what puts you in the process; the email is what puts a name to it. A sensible rhythm is to let the app work through the job sites on the roles that match, and to reserve Cold Mail for the shortlist you would take a call about tonight: the jobs where you would genuinely like a human to know who you are.

If you are still building that shortlist, the guide to searching jobs and one-click apply shows how to fill a queue worth writing about. The two features are designed as a pair, and the totals on the Dashboard count both.

The pattern is the same for job seekers in India and the United States. The sites differ, LinkedIn and Indeed in the United States, Naukri and Internshala in India, but the habit is identical: find the person attached to the job, add them with a work email and a profile link, and write to them like a person.

Cold Mail is not for mailing everyone at a company, and it is not a replacement for applying. One thoughtful message to the person who posted the job, after your application is in, is the whole play.

Your first batch: a checklist

  1. Fill in your profile first. Drafts pull from it, and an empty field becomes a vague sentence.
  2. Connect Gmail and sign in with the one account you want to send from.
  3. Add recipients one per line, email first, with a work email and a profile link wherever you can.
  4. Keep the list to people on jobs you have found or applied to, and to a number you will actually review.
  5. Read the research bullets before the body. Fix wrong premises with Edit, or skip the draft.
  6. Leave attachments off for first messages. Offer the resume in the reply instead.
  7. Approve one draft at a time. There is no other way, by design.
  8. When someone replies with an opt-out, let the app record it and move on. They will not hear from you again.

That is the whole loop: choose the person, review the draft, send the mail, and stay welcome in the inbox. Cold Mail is one part of the app, and the guide to getting the most out of GMAFJ shows how it fits with everything else.
