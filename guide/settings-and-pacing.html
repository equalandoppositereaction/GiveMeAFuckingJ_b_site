GMAFJ Settings: Pacing, Employer Accounts and More

What each setting does, how to choose your pacing, and how employer account details help on company career sites.

Settings is the room in GMAFJ where you decide how the app behaves: how quickly it applies, what it looks like on your screen, and how your accounts appear to the app. It is deliberately small, because the choices that shape your applications most, such as your job preferences and the sites for each run, live on the Dashboard instead. This guide walks through everything the settings screen holds, suggests a calm first-week pass through it, and explains the two parts that deserve the most thought: pacing and your accounts.

What the settings screen holds

You find Settings in the footer of the sidebar: a small gear beside your name and photo. Your name opens the Account page; the gear beside it opens Settings. If you run the sidebar collapsed, the two stack together in the same corner.

The screen holds four things: pacing, the theme, your accounts, and sign-out. That is the whole list. GMAFJ keeps its settings few on purpose, because most of what decides an application is set elsewhere: the titles, locations and filters that build the queue live in your profile and on the Apply through job sites card, and the answers the app gives live in the Answers library. If you catch yourself hunting for one of those, it is not in Settings. Most sections in the app carry a small question mark at their edge that explains what the section does, and Settings is no exception, so you can read up in place without leaving the screen.

The accounts part is worth a second look. For each job site you have signed in to, it shows which account that is, by name, so a work login and a personal one never get mixed up. The sign-ins themselves are not managed here: you sign in on each site's own page in the app's browser, starting from Profile on Job Sites in the sidebar, which the guide to signing in to job sites covers step by step.

A first-week pass through settings

Settings rewards one slow look in the first week and very little attention after that. Here is a pass that fits around your first runs.

Day one: look, do not turn

Open Settings once and read it top to bottom. Pick your theme if you care about one; the app starts on System, which follows your Windows light or dark setting. Read the pacing section and glance at the accounts list to confirm that the site you signed into during setup shows the account you expect. Change nothing else yet.

Your first runs: leave pacing alone

The pace you start with is conservative on purpose, because the app works inside your own accounts and behaves the way a careful person would. For your first few runs, leave it exactly where it is. Watch one run end to end, or part of one, so you know what the rhythm looks like and what a pause looks like when it happens; the guide to applying through job sites explains what you will see.

End of the first week: adjust on evidence

After a handful of runs you have something better than an opinion: results. If every run has completed with no pauses from the sites, and the queue keeps holding more good postings than a run gets through, that is the moment to consider a quicker pace, one small step at a time. If any site has paused the queue, keep that site where it is. The next two sections give the detail.

Pacing: what it controls

Pacing is the rhythm of a run: how much time passes between one application and the next, on each site. It is set per site, so a run covering two boards keeps a separate rhythm for each, inside each site's own account. You set it in Settings, and it is your choice; the app does not quietly speed up when you look away or slow down when you are watching.

Three details make the rhythm safe rather than merely slow. The gap is measured from the moment one application is sent to the moment the next one opens, so a long form does not earn a shorter wait. The app counts what it has sent on each site over a rolling day rather than resetting at midnight, which matches how the sites themselves count. And the app fills forms at a natural typing pace instead of pasting text in instantly, because machine-speed forms are exactly the thing job sites are built to notice.

Pacing also sits on top of something firmer: the app sends one application at a time, finishing each before the next starts. There is no fixed daily limit on how many jobs it can apply to. What shapes a day's volume is the pace you set, how many sites the run covers, and how many matching jobs the search finds.

One boundary helps to keep in mind: pacing does not decide which jobs enter the queue. Your preferences do. A well-aimed search at a steady pace beats a wide search at any pace, because every posting in the queue becomes a complete application sent under your name.

Choosing a pace that is effective and safe

The instinct is to go fast, and the honest answer is that a faster pace mostly buys you pauses rather than throughput. Job sites watch for machine-speed activity, and when a site pushes back, the app stops and waits for you: the queue pauses with the site's name and a suggested wait, and nothing resumes until you say so. A wait like that costs more than the seconds a quicker pace saved. In practice, the steady setting is usually the fast one.

How to raise the pace without trouble

  • Start where the app starts. The pace you begin with is the app's own recommendation. Run with it for your first week.
  • Raise one site at a time, in small steps. Pacing is per site, so you can give the board that carries most of your matching jobs a little more speed and leave the rest alone.
  • Change between runs, not during one. Let a run finish, look at the results, then adjust before the next one.
  • Let clean runs be the only evidence. A run that completes with no pause cards is the only sign that a site is comfortable.

When to slow down

  • A site paused the queue. If a site tells the app it is going too fast, treat that as the site speaking. Leave that site's pace alone, or ease it back, and let the next few runs pass without friction.
  • The account is new. A profile created last week is treated differently from an established one, on every site. Give new accounts the gentlest pace for a while.
  • Challenges or sign-outs repeat. A site that keeps showing puzzles or ending your session is telling you something. Slower is the answer that works.

When a quicker pace is reasonable

  • Weeks of clean runs. If runs complete with no pauses and no challenges, a small increase is a reasonable experiment.
  • A deep queue. Pace only matters when there are more good postings than the day allows. If the queue comes back short, pacing is not your bottleneck; the search is.

Where the volume sits depends on where you are hunting. In India, runs usually centre on Naukri, with Internshala alongside for internships, so those are the accounts whose pace decides most of your week. In the United States, LinkedIn, Indeed, ZipRecruiter and Glassdoor carry most of the volume. Set each site's pace to suit that site and its account, not to one global idea of speed.

Employer accounts on company career pages

Job boards are one sign-in each, covering many employers at once. Company career pages are the opposite: each employer's own site, reached job by job when a posting hands over from a board. Many of those pages take you straight to the form, and the app fills it like any other, so plenty of postings never raise the subject of an account at all. Some employers, though, want an account on their own careers portal before you can apply, and often the whole gate is a single box asking for your email and a Continue button.

What the app does at that gate

The app does not create accounts for you, anywhere. When it reaches an employer's sign-up, it sets that posting aside and keeps the link to the page, so you can open it when it suits you, create the account or sign in, and complete that application yourself. The posting is not dropped quietly: you will see it, with the reason, alongside the other things the app left for you.

When they help, and how to think about them

An employer account earns its keep the moment it exists, because that wall is gone for every future posting at the same employer. Whether it is worth creating is your call. A role you genuinely want at a company with a portal is usually worth a few minutes; a borderline posting rarely is.

Think of these accounts the way you think of the accounts on the boards. They are yours, created by you on the employer's own page, and they sit with the employer, not with GMAFJ. The app works inside them in your own session, the same as everywhere else. And it holds one boundary on every page, employer sign-ups included: it never types a password, a government ID number, or bank or card details into any form. Those fields stay yours, which is also why an application asking for that kind of detail is treated as a warning sign rather than a form to fill. Your sessions, on boards and employer sites alike, stay on your computer, and the app never receives your passwords.

Because accounts and sessions live on your machine, it helps to know what the app claims and what it does not. It is plain in Settings that no app can protect your whole computer for you: encrypting your disk is your responsibility, and the app says so rather than implying otherwise. If you ever want everything the app kept on the machine gone in one move, there is an Erase all local data action that clears it, signed-in sessions included.

Theme, sign-out and the small settings

The theme has three choices: System, Light and Dark, with System as the default. On System the app follows your Windows setting, so it goes dark when Windows does. If you flip themes often, you do not need Settings for it: Toggle theme is one of the commands in the command palette, which opens from the search bar at the top of the window.

Sign-out does what it says: it signs you out of your GMAFJ account, the one you created with your email address. It is also available as a Sign out command in the palette. Your job site sign-ins are a separate matter and are not handled through it. They live in the sites' own sessions, and Profile on Job Sites is where you manage those, including signing in again when a site ends one.

Answers are managed in the library, not in Settings

There are no switches in Settings for how the app answers questions, because the answers themselves are the setting. Every answer the app gives is saved, and the Answers library in the sidebar is where you work with them: search them, sort them, edit any answer, delete one, and decide whether it should be reused everywhere or kept to the job it was given for.

Two moments are worth knowing in advance. The first is the one decision you make inside a run: when you answer a question yourself, the app asks one thing before saving it.

Will this answer be the same for other jobs?

Answer yes and the app reuses it wherever the same question appears; answer no and it stays with that job only. The second moment is the audit. When you want to check what the app has been saying on your behalf, the library's filters, such as the one for AI-answered questions, pull up exactly those answers. A wrong answer saved once would otherwise be repeated politely forever, so a glance at the library now and then is the habit that matters. The guide to reviewing your answers goes through all of it in full.

When to change settings and when to leave them

The pattern that works: change a setting when a run has given you a reason, not before. A completed run with no pauses is the reason to try a quicker pace. A pause from a site is the reason to leave that site alone. A new board in your mix is the reason to visit the accounts list and confirm the right profile is showing. The theme is the exception; it is yours to change on a whim.

The negative space matters just as much. Most problems people hit are not settings problems: a site that will not sign in, a queue that comes back empty, a posting that froze on a question. Those have their own causes and their own fixes, which is what the troubleshooting guide is for. Turning knobs in Settings to fix an unrelated problem usually just adds a second thing to undo.

A settings checklist

  • I know where Settings lives: the gear beside my name in the sidebar footer.
  • I have read the pacing section and left the defaults for my first runs.
  • The accounts list shows the account I expect for every site I signed into.
  • My theme is set the way I like it, or left on System so it follows Windows.
  • I know the rule for pace changes: small steps, one site at a time, between runs, on evidence.
  • I know what an employer account is for, and that the app never creates one for me.
  • I know the app never types passwords, ID numbers or bank details, on any site.
  • I have opened the Answers library once to see what the app has been saving.

That is the whole screen. Set it once with a little care, then let the runs hand you the evidence for any change after that. Settings will have done its job the way the rest of the app does: quietly, in the background, while you get on with the job hunt itself.
