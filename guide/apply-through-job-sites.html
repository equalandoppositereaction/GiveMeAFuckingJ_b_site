How to Apply Through Job Sites With GMAFJ

Choosing your job sites, titles and locations, starting a run, watching it work, and stopping it.

One session of applying through your job sites is called a run. GMAFJ searches the sites you are signed in to for postings that match your preferences, opens each one, fills in the application, and submits it, one posting at a time, while you get on with your day. This guide covers the choices that shape a run before you start it, what the app does while it works, and what to do at the moments it stops and asks for you.

Pick the job sites for the run

GMAFJ applies on LinkedIn (Easy Apply), Indeed, Naukri, Internshala, Glassdoor, ZipRecruiter, Monster, Foundit and Wellfound, and on company career pages, including those built on Greenhouse, Lever and Ashby. That is the full list, and it is longer than the two or three sites most people actually need. Which sites a run uses is your choice, and you make it fresh each time.

You choose on the Apply through job sites card on the Dashboard. Under Select the job sites to apply on, the sites you are signed into come first, and the rest sit greyed out at the end of the list, because a site you are not signed into cannot be part of a run. Hover over a greyed out one and it tells you what to do: sign in on Profile on Job Sites. Signing in takes about a minute per site, and the guide to signing in to job sites walks through it step by step.

Pick the sites that carry the jobs you actually want, and one is enough to get going. If you are job hunting in India, that will usually be Naukri, with Internshala if you are looking for internships and Foundit alongside them. In the United States, LinkedIn, Indeed, ZipRecruiter and Glassdoor carry most of the volume, and company career pages cover much of the rest.

Resist the urge to sign in everywhere and select everything. Each site in a run is one of your accounts the app works through, and a site with few postings that fit you mostly adds time between the applications you care about. Three well-chosen sites beat nine not because the queue is shorter but because it is better: every posting in it came from a place that actually carries your kind of job.

Company career pages work differently from the boards. They are not signed into in advance and cannot be picked on the card. Instead, when a posting leads from a board to the employer's own site, the app follows it there and completes the application on that page. Some employers want an account on their own site before you can apply; the app does not create accounts for you, so it sets that posting aside and keeps the link for you rather than dropping it quietly. Some boards also carry their own extra search filters, which sit behind a More filters menu on the card whenever the site offers them.

Choose titles and locations that fit

The search inside a run uses your job preferences: titles, locations, on-site, hybrid or remote, seniority, a minimum salary if you set one, and companies to exclude. Those preferences decide what lands in the queue, which makes them the most powerful settings in the app. An evening spent on them is repaid on every run after it.

For titles, use the words employers advertise with, not the one you use in your head, and include a few close variants. The same work is advertised as Backend Developer, Backend Engineer and Platform Engineer depending on who wrote the listing. Then stop. A pile of loosely related titles does not widen your options; it fills the queue with jobs you would never read, and every one of those still becomes a complete application sent under your name.

Too wide: Developer, Engineer, Consultant, Analyst, Manager, Architect. Right-sized: Backend Developer, Backend Engineer, Platform Engineer.

Locations work the same way. List the cities and regions where you would genuinely accept an offer. In India that might be Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Pune; in the United States, the metro areas within commuting or moving distance. Remote is its own preference, separate from the location list: if you only want remote work, say so there and let the locations stay broad.

Two preferences quietly improve a queue more than people expect. The minimum salary is the floor below which you would turn the job down, and the app skips listings under it, so enter the number you mean rather than the one you hope for. The exclude list is for employers you never want to hear from again, such as places that have already rejected you. Set both once, and every future run inherits them.

A focused search is not about applying to fewer jobs overall. It is about making every application one you would defend in an interview. If you want more volume, the honest way to get it is more genuine variants of the roles you want, across the sites that carry them, which is a different thing from casting a huge net; how many jobs to apply to covers that balance in depth. And if you would rather browse and pick postings yourself, the GMAFJ website lists thousands of jobs you can search by title, filter and open in the app to apply with one click, which searching jobs and one click apply explains.

Start a run

Before a run starts, the app checks two things. The first is your profile: a short list of essentials every posting asks for, such as your name, your email, your phone number with its country code, the countries you can work in, and whether you need visa sponsorship. If anything is missing, the app names it and explains why it will not start, because an application without those stops at the first page with no explanation. The second is your resume, which is attached to every application the run sends.

Sign-ins are treated as information rather than a wall. If a site you picked looks signed out, the app tells you before the run starts and offers to open that site's sign-in page, but it still lets you start, because the site may accept the session after all. If it does not, the run pauses at that point and asks you to sign in, which the section below covers.

When you press Start applying, the app searches the sites you selected and builds the queue: the postings that match your preferences, ready to be worked through in order. Look down the queue before letting it loose. You can remove any posting you would rather not apply to, and the run starts with whatever remains.

The queue holds only fresh postings. The app remembers what it has already applied to and does not send a second application to the same posting, so last week's run does not come back to haunt this one. If a search turns up little that matches, the queue is simply short; broadening one title or one location is the fix, and you can see the effect on the very next run.

Watch the run work

A run does its work in the background. The app drives its own built-in browser with the view parked out of sight, so you can minimise the window and get on with your day. The Now running card on the Dashboard shows the current job, the company and the live phase, with Watch live beside it, and a small dot appears on the Browser item in the sidebar while the run goes unwatched. The Apply through job sites card wears a green outline of its own while the run is active.

Press Watch live and you can look over its shoulder. The app searches, opens a posting, works through the form page by page, and submits. It types at a natural pace, reads back what it typed to be sure each field took it, fills what it can from your profile, and skips optional questions it has no answer for. When a form asks for a cover letter, the app writes one for that role and attaches it as a PDF. Your resume goes out with every application.

What you are watching are the sites' real pages, loaded in the app's browser, in your own logged-in session. The site asks the app exactly what it would ask you, which is why the occasional pause looks like something you would have seen applying by hand. A strip above the page names the phase and where the run stands, something like filling application 3 of 12, with the job's title and company beside it. The stop button on that strip stops the watching, not the run: leave it and the app keeps working.

Runs are serial by design. The app sends one application at a time and starts the next only when the current one is finished, because that is what keeps the pace human and the answers landing in the right forms. An application counts as sent when the site itself shows its confirmation, not when the button is clicked. On some boards a single button sends the application immediately; even there, the app presses it once and waits for the site's confirmation before counting it.

Results appear as the run goes. The Applications board on the Dashboard lists one row per job, newest first, carrying the logo of the site it went in through: a green outline means sent, a yellow outline means the application is frozen on a question and waiting for you. Postings the app passed over do not appear on the board. The reasons it skipped them are shown together in plain sentences, such as a posting needing an account you have not created, so you can act on them in one go rather than one by one.

Pause, stop and close

You can pause a run at any time from the command palette, which opens from the search bar at the top of the window, and start the queue again from the same place. You can also stop a run altogether. Neither cuts an application off midway: the application in flight finishes, and nothing new starts after it. There is no half-filled form left behind on some employer's site.

Closing the app is safe too. If a question was waiting for you when you closed, that check is still there the next time you open the app. The browser session itself does not survive closing, so when you resume, the app reopens the posting and refills the form from your profile and saved answers, including the answer you gave before closing.

One distinction is worth knowing in advance. Pausing or stopping on purpose is yours to do whenever you like. When a job site itself slows the run down, the app does not resume on its own; that is a different kind of stop, and it is the subject of the next section.

When the run stops and asks for you

Most of a run needs nothing from you, and the FAQ is open about it: applications may be submitted without you reviewing each answer. What the app will not do is guess on the small set of things that genuinely need a human. For those it stops and asks, and these stops are called checks. Knowing the common kinds means none of them will surprise you.

What a check looks like

A check is a card at the top of the Dashboard, above everything else, because the queue waits on it. The card shows a picture of the question, the job's title and company, and the question written out as text so you can read and copy it. While the card is open, no other application starts, because the app works on one posting at a time and will not leave you answering a form that has moved on.

The common kinds

  • A question only you can answer. The app answers form questions from your profile, and where the profile has no answer it gives the answer you would most likely give. Some questions it declines to answer on your behalf: a consent box, an agreement, or a country-specific work authorisation question it cannot answer honestly from the countries in your profile. Those come to you.
  • Consent and terms boxes. Agreements are yours to give, so the app leaves them unticked and asks rather than accepting for you.
  • A sign-in that expired mid-run. The whole queue pauses, a banner explains it, and you sign in again the way you always do. The run picks back up afterwards.
  • An employer sign-up. Some employers want an account on their own career site before you can apply. The app does not create accounts on your behalf; it parks the posting and hands you the link, and you decide whether it is worth it.

Answering a check

Answering one is deliberately easy. The app brings the page forward, highlights the one question it needs, and everything else on the form is already filled. Answer it in the app's browser, press the continue button on the strip above the page, and the app reads the field back, asks whether the answer would work for other jobs too, saves it, and carries on. The saved answer is reused from then on, so most checks happen once. If you would rather not answer, Skip this job sets that posting aside and the run continues with the next.

When a site itself stops the run

Two pauses are not about questions at all. If a site shows a puzzle or a challenge to confirm a human is present, the queue pauses and the page is left to you: solve it as you would anywhere else, because the app never attempts to solve or bypass these checks. And if a site indicates the app is going too fast, the queue pauses with the site's name and a suggested wait, and the app waits for you to resume rather than pushing on. Both pauses exist to protect your account, and both clear in a moment.

One last case, rarer still: if a page looks so different from what the app expects that it cannot tell what is on it, the app leaves that one posting behind with the reason and moves on, and pauses the run only if it happens on several postings in a row. A network hiccup is retried, and if it still fails, that posting is marked failed and the queue continues. Every kind of stop, and how to answer each check well, is covered in what to do when a job needs you.

Choose a pace that is effective and safe

Pacing is the rhythm of a run: how much time passes between one application and the next on each site. You set it in Settings. The defaults are conservative on purpose, because the app works inside your accounts, and a burst of machine-speed activity is exactly what job sites are built to notice.

In practice, the app keeps a gap between applications on each site, counts what it has sent on that site over a rolling day rather than resetting at midnight, and measures the gap from the moment one application is sent to the moment the next one opens, so a long form does not earn a shorter wait. It also fills forms at a natural pace rather than instantly. None of this needs your attention; it is simply why a run looks unhurried if you watch it.

There is no fixed daily limit on how many jobs the app can apply to. What shapes a day's volume is the pace you set, how many sites the run covers, and how many matching jobs the search finds. If you raise the pace, raise it in small steps and watch how the sites respond. A faster pace mostly buys you pauses rather than throughput: the moment a site pushes back, the app stops anyway and waits for you, and that wait costs more than the seconds you saved. The steady setting is usually the fast one.

Pacing is one of several habits built into the app that keep your accounts in good standing. It works only in your own logged-in sessions, sends one application at a time, never types a password or a government ID, tax, bank or card number into any form, and unticks marketing boxes that arrive pre-ticked rather than ever ticking anything itself. Your sign-ins stay on your computer throughout, and the app never receives your passwords.

Habits that make runs better

A run is only as good as what you feed it, and a few habits cover most of the difference.

  • Refresh the inputs before you run. A new skill, a finished project, a changed notice period, an updated resume: put it in the profile first, and every application that day carries it.
  • Keep your first runs small. Trim the queue to a handful of postings, watch one run end to end, and look at the applications on the board. Once you like what you see, let the bigger runs loose.
  • Be around at the start. A run with an open check waits for you, so start one when you will be reachable for the first little while. Checks get rarer as your saved answers build up.
  • Review after each run. Read the board, read why postings were skipped, and glance at the totals on the Dashboard, which count applications sent with a this-week figure beside them. Open the site itself now and then too: applications appear in each site's own history as well.
  • Audit your saved answers. Every answer the app gives is stored, and the Answers library lets you search, edit and delete them. A wrong answer saved once would otherwise be reused politely forever.
  • Tighten preferences when the fit drifts. If a run applies to jobs you would not read, fix a title or drop a location and run again. That loop is your main hand on the quality of everything.
  • Expect to sign in again occasionally. Sessions expire for ordinary reasons. Signing in again from Profile on Job Sites takes a minute and needs nothing else from the app.

Your next run, in one list

  1. Check that your profile essentials and your resume are current.
  2. Sign in to the job sites that carry the jobs you want, starting with one.
  3. Set titles, locations, remote preference, seniority, salary floor and exclusions to what you would genuinely accept.
  4. On the Apply through job sites card, pick the sites for this run.
  5. Look down the queue and remove anything you would not defend in an interview.
  6. Press Start applying, then get on with your day.
  7. Answer checks when they appear; everything else on the form is already filled.
  8. Afterwards, read the board, the skip reasons and your saved answers, and adjust before the next run.
