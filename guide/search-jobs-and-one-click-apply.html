How to Search Jobs and Use One Click Apply

Finding jobs on the dashboard, opening them to apply yourself, and letting GMAFJ apply for you with one click.

GMAFJ gives you two ways to get an application out. You can find a job yourself in the Search jobs card on the Dashboard and deal with it the moment you have read it, or you can hand the job to the app with One click apply and let it fill in and submit the form for you. This guide covers both paths: how to search the listings so they come back with jobs worth reading, how to read a listing before you spend an application on it, what each button actually does, and how to keep the quality of your applications up while the volume goes up.

Where the jobs are

The Dashboard is the screen the app opens on, and the Search jobs card sits on it, just under the dial that counts the applications you have sent. The card holds every job listed on GMAFJ's website, thousands of postings at any time. The same listings, search and filters sit on the website's home page, so you can hunt from a phone or a friend's laptop; the two buttons this guide is about live in the app. If you have not installed the app yet, the download page has it.

The card stays quiet until you ask it something. Nothing shows until you have typed a title or picked a filter, so there is no wall of jobs to scroll past before you have said what you want. Type a title and the results stream in, newest postings first, with a line above them telling you how many jobs have matched so far. Press Show more jobs at the bottom when you want the next batch.

Five filters narrow the search, and each does exactly what it says:

  • Location. Type a city or a country. As you type, the box suggests the countries and cities the listings actually use, which saves you guessing at spellings.
  • Remote or on-site. Remote, On-site, or either.
  • Any job type. Full time, Part time, Contract, Internship, Temporary, Freelance or Apprenticeship.
  • Any experience. Entry level, Mid level, Senior level or Executive level.
  • Posted any time. Past 24 hours, Past 3 days, Past week or Past month.

Search the way employers write

The title box matches words in the posting's title, so search with the words employers actually advertise with, not the word your last employer used internally. The same work turns up under several names, and each variant you add is a set of listings the others miss.

Accounts Executive, Accounts Assistant, Junior Accountant

Keep the list close. A handful of variants brings back every posting worth reading; a pile of loosely related titles brings back a pile you would never open. If the results come back full of tiles you would not click on, tighten the title before you widen anything else.

For locations, list the places you would genuinely work in. In India that might be Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Pune; in the United States, the metro areas within commuting or moving distance. If you only want remote work, pick Remote and leave the location box alone, because remote postings are not tied to a city.

What a listing shows

Each result is a tile, and the top of it answers the first three questions you would ask anyway: what the job is, where it is, and what it pays. A tile carries the job title, the location (or "Location not listed"), whether it is remote or on-site and of what type, and the salary when the posting gives one. Pay shows in the posting's own currency, so an Indian listing reads in lakhs and an American one in thousands of dollars a year; "Salary not listed" means the posting did not say.

Click anywhere on the top of a tile and it opens in place, growing to show the details without losing your place in the results: the day it was posted, the experience and education it asks for, the language the posting is in, and, where the listing has them, the tasks under "What you will do", the skills, the perks and the roles on offer. Some listings carry only a short summary. For those, the tile says so, and the full description waits on the job's own page.

Read the listing before you spend an application on it

Applying is cheap with GMAFJ, which is precisely why a half minute of reading is worth it first: every application sent to a job you would never take is queue time you did not spend on one you would. Before you touch either button, give the opened tile a short pass:

  • The title. Does the job on the tile match the job in your head? Employers dress titles up and down, and "Executive" in India often means a junior role rather than a senior one.
  • The date. The posted filter can keep stale listings out of your search in the first place; on the tile, the posting date tells you whether the role is fresh or has been open a while.
  • Experience and education. The listing states both when it states them at all. Read the line rather than the label, because "entry level" means one thing to you and another to the poster.
  • The skills. This is the fastest fit test there is. If the listed skills are mostly things you have, or things you can defend, the job is worth an application. If two skills make up the whole job and you have neither, move on.
  • What you will do. The task list tells you the day to day, which titles never do. Two jobs with the same title can be different work.
  • The pay. Where the posting names a salary, decide now whether it clears your floor, so you are not deciding it during an offer call three months from now.

One more habit, in India especially: a posting that asks you to pay a fee, buy training, or hand over bank details in order to apply is not a job. GMAFJ will not fill in bank details or government ID numbers on any form; treat a listing that leads with money the same way, and skip it.

Apply yourself to a job you choose

When you have read a listing and you want this job, press Apply on the tile. GMAFJ opens the job's page in its own built-in browser, and the application form is in front of you. Nothing is filled in for you on this path: you are applying with your own hands, in your own logged-in session, on the page the employer actually hosts.

The steps are the ordinary ones, with a couple of points worth making:

  1. Read the full posting. The job's page carries the complete description even when the listing only summarised it. Skim the responsibilities and the requirements before the form.
  2. Sign in if the site asks. The browser is the app's, but the session is yours, and your password never passes through the app. If a sign-in will not go through here, sign in to that site from Profile on Job Sites and open the job again.
  3. Fill the form properly. This is the path to use when the answers matter, so write the long answers yourself rather than pasting something generic.
  4. Attach the resume you want read. If the role matters, use a resume shaped for this description, not a general one.
  5. Submit. The application is done, and the job is kept in your applications history so you can tell later which listings you already handled yourself.

Applying yourself is slower, and it is the right choice for the jobs where slow is the point: the role you want most, a form full of questions that decide the shortlist, an employer you have been circling for months. For one of those, it pays to shape the resume first, and the guide to tailoring your resume to the job description covers what actually to change.

One click apply: what happens when you press it

Every tile has a second button: One click apply. Where Apply hands the job to you, One click apply hands the job to the app. Press it and the job joins a queue; the app opens the job's page, fills in the application, and submits it, one job at a time. You do not have to watch the form unless you want to.

The button tells you where the job is the whole way through. It reads Queuing… while the job is being taken in, then Queued, then Applying… while the app works, and finally Applied once the site itself has confirmed the application went through. The app counts nothing as sent on a click alone; the page has to say so first.

While the queue works, you can keep searching, open tiles, and queue more jobs. The One click queue works one job at a time, separately from any run you have started on the job boards, so the two never collide. The app waits between applications at the pace you set in Settings; the wait exists to keep your accounts safe, so leave it alone while you are new. The Stop button stops everything that is applying, the One click queue included, and the job it was on stays queued for next time.

One detail worth knowing: once a tile shows Applied, pressing One click apply on it again does nothing. The app will not send a second application to a posting it has already sent one to.

What the app fills in

Everything the form asks for that your profile can answer, it fills from the profile: your name and contact details, your work eligibility, your education, your experience, your skills and your years per skill. In India that includes the fields Indian applications always ask, notice period and current and expected CTC; in the United States, the work authorisation and sponsorship questions. This is why a complete profile matters more than anything else you can do: the app can only send what the profile holds.

The written questions, the ones no profile can hold, the app answers with AI, drawing on your profile and the job description, and it writes a cover letter tailored to the role whenever a form asks for one. Your resume goes out with the application. Every answer, whether the app's or yours, is saved in the answers library and reused the next time the same question appears, which is why the tenth application goes faster than the first.

Two things the app will not do, on any form: it will not fill in passwords, government ID numbers or bank details, and it will not pick an option that is not true for you. If a required question has no answer that is true, the app stops rather than squeeze through with a lie.

The other states the button can show

Applied is the happy ending, and the button has three more words it can hold:

  • Needs you. A question came up that the app will not guess on, so the job is held and its question is brought to the Dashboard, next to the job it belongs to. Answer it there, or skip the job. Answering the last open question on a job puts the job back in the queue to be applied to again, and if you tell the app the answer will be the same for other jobs, it saves the answer and never asks you that question again. Sometimes this state means the form was submitted but the page never confirmed it; the job is held rather than counted, so nothing is lost by looking.
  • Not eligible. The employer's own form said you do not fit: a drop-down where no option is true for you, or the site's own notice that you are ineligible to apply. The app reports that instead of picking a false answer, and moves on to the next job in the queue.
  • Try again. The attempt failed for a reason that may not exist the next time round: the page would not open, or something interrupted it. Press the button and the job is queued once more.

A pause with a banner across the Dashboard is a different thing from a single job failing. An expired sign-in or a security check stops everything until you deal with it, because the app will not push into a site that is pushing back. Sign in again or solve the check in the app's browser, and the queue carries on where it left off.

When to apply yourself, and when to use One click apply

Both buttons send one application to one job you chose. The difference is who spends the effort, and the honest answer is that you will want both in the same week.

  • Apply yourself when the job is worth an hour: the role you actually want, a form with written questions that decide the shortlist, an employer whose page you want to read in full first, or any application where you want the resume shaped to the description before anything goes out.
  • One click apply when the job is a solid fit but not a prize worth an hour: the posting matches your profile, the tile answered your questions, and you would rather spend your evening on the five jobs you care about than on fifty forms you do not. Also when you are deliberately widening the net, because volume is part of how job searches end.

A rhythm that works: read the search results once in the morning, press Apply on the two or three jobs you would take on Monday, and press One click apply on the rest that cleared the bar. Then let the queue run while you do something else. The third way, pressing Start applying and letting the app search the job sites for you using your preferences, is a different mode again, and it has its own guide to applying through job sites.

Keeping quality up while you move fast

One click apply takes the typing out of applying. It does not take the judgement out, and the judgement is what employers still read. Three habits keep the quality where you want it while the volume climbs.

Review your saved answers

Every answer the app gives is saved, searchable and editable in the Answers library in the sidebar, with the question it answers, where it came from and how many times it has been used. Applications may go out without you reviewing each answer first, which is exactly why the library is worth a visit after your first batch. Filter for the AI-answered ones, read them as though an applicant had written them to you, and fix anything you would phrase differently: edit an answer once and every future application uses your version. The guide to reviewing your answers walks through this in full.

Keep the search tight

A search that returns everything returns nothing usable. If a page of tiles is mostly jobs you would not open, sharpen the title words, set the experience filter honestly, and use the posted filter so old listings stay out. Ten results worth reading beat a hundred worth skipping, in this card as everywhere else.

Keep the profile and resume current

The profile is where every filled field comes from, so a stale profile sends stale applications: an old employer, a skill you have since learned, an expected salary you have since revised. Update the profile as things change and every future application inherits the update. The guide to building a complete profile covers it part by part. For the few jobs that matter most, shape the resume to each description before you press anything, and let One click apply carry the rest.

The checklist

Every time you sit down to the Search jobs card:

  1. Search by the title the way employers write it, and include your variants.
  2. Set the location, remote or on-site, job type, experience and posted filters before you read anything.
  3. Open the tiles that survive, and check the date, the experience line, the skills and the pay.
  4. Press Apply for the jobs you want most, and fill those forms yourself, with a resume shaped to each description.
  5. Press One click apply on the rest that cleared the bar, and let the queue work at its own pace.
  6. Deal with anything marked Needs you the same day, so the queue does not wait on you overnight.
  7. After the batch, open Answers, filter for AI-answered, and correct anything you would phrase differently.
  8. Update your profile whenever it changes, because the next application is filled from it.

That is the whole loop: search, read, choose, and let the app carry the forms. One click apply is at its best when the profile behind it is complete and the answers behind it are yours, so the time it saves is time you can spend on the handful of applications that deserve the long way round.
