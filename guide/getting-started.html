Getting Started With GMAFJ

Install GMAFJ, create your account, build your profile from your resume and sign in to your first job site.

GMAFJ is a free Windows app that finds jobs matching your profile and applies to them for you. You set it up once: a profile, a resume, and a sign-in to the job sites you already use. After that the app fills in and submits the applications, remembers every answer it gives, and brings you only the things that genuinely need a human. This guide takes you from installing the app to your first run, and tells you what to expect in the first few days.

What GMAFJ is, and who it is for

GMAFJ, short for GiveMeAFuckingJ*b, is a desktop app for Windows. It searches the job sites you choose, opens the postings that match your profile, fills in the application forms, and submits them. It answers each form's questions from your profile, attaches your resume, writes a cover letter when a form asks for one, and saves every answer so the next application is faster than the last.

It is free. Every feature is included, cold mail too, and there is no subscription. The app sends one application at a time, one after another, at the pace you set, for as long as it keeps finding jobs that match.

It applies on LinkedIn (Easy Apply), Indeed, Naukri, Internshala, Glassdoor, ZipRecruiter, Monster, Foundit and Wellfound, and on company career pages, including those built on Greenhouse, Lever and Ashby. If you are job hunting in India, Naukri, Internshala and Foundit are covered. In the United States, LinkedIn, Indeed, ZipRecruiter and Glassdoor carry most of the volume, and the career pages cover much of the rest.

It works for freshers and experienced people alike, for internships and entry-level roles as well as senior ones. What changes is what you put in your profile, not how you use the app. The app also drafts cold emails to the recruiters and hiring managers you choose, from your own Gmail account, and sends each one only after you approve it. That part is called Cold Mail, and it has its own page in the app.

Separately, the GMAFJ website lists thousands of jobs you can search by title and filter by location, remote or on-site, job type, experience and posting date. Open any of them in the desktop app and you can apply with one click.

Install GMAFJ on Windows

GMAFJ runs on Windows 10 and Windows 11 and installs from the Microsoft Store. The download page takes you to the right place. Installation needs no configuration: install it, open it, and the app appears once it has finished loading.

The app ships with its own browser built into it, and that is the key to how it works. You sign in to your job sites inside that browser, exactly as you would in Chrome or Edge. The app then drives the application forms through it, in your own logged-in session, on your own computer. Your sign-ins never leave your machine, and GMAFJ never receives your passwords.

A Mac version is coming soon. There is no Linux or mobile version at the moment.

Create your free account

Open the app and create an account with your email and a password, or sign in if you already have one. The first time the app runs on a computer it offers a quick tour. Take it if you want the shape of the place, or skip it; both paths end on the Dashboard, which is the screen you will use every day.

The Dashboard tells you what to do next at each stage of setup. When your profile and your first sign-in are done, its message is short: "All done! Just put in your job requirements and click Start applying." The rest of this guide is those steps, in order.

Build your profile

The profile is the most important thing you will fill in today. The app answers application questions from it, so every field you complete is a field it can fill on every future application without asking you. Nothing you enter here is used once and thrown away.

Fill it in, or upload your resume

The profile opens as a short wizard, and you have two ways through it: fill in the fields yourself, or upload your resume and work from that. You can do both, typing the parts your resume does not cover. Each step saves as you go, so quitting halfway loses nothing, and a rail beside the form lets you jump between sections.

The sections cover your identity and contact details, links such as LinkedIn and a portfolio, education, experience, projects and skills, work eligibility, a set of voluntary reporting questions, and your job preferences. Most of it is optional. The parts that are not are the basics every posting asks for, and the app is upfront about them.

Before your first run the app checks those basics and names anything that is missing, with the reason: without them an application stops at the first page with no explanation. The usual list is short: your name, your email, your phone number with its country code, the countries you can work in, and whether you need visa sponsorship. Fill those in and the gate is open.

The sections that matter most

Work eligibility is where India and the United States differ. In India, forms ask for your notice period and your current and expected CTC, so enter real numbers: your contractual notice, not the hopeful one. In the United States, forms ask whether you are authorised to work in the country and whether you will need visa sponsorship, now or in the future, so make sure the countries you can work in and the sponsorship answer are correct before your first run.

The voluntary questions, gender and similar reporting categories, are all optional and every one offers "Prefer not to say". Answer them or leave them, as you like. One section deserves particular care: years of experience per skill. Forms ask this constantly and the app cannot work it out for you, because five years of total experience might be one year of SQL. Freshers, put your course and internship tools here; small numbers are fine, and honest.

Your job preferences come last: titles, locations, on-site, hybrid or remote, seniority, a minimum salary if you want to set one, and companies you want to exclude. They matter enough to get their own section below. For a section-by-section walkthrough of the whole profile, read how to build a complete profile.

Your resume

Every application needs a resume attached, and the app gives you two routes: it can generate a clean PDF resume from your profile, or you can upload your own. You can do both and keep whichever you prefer, and there is a live preview before anything is saved. Whichever you choose, that PDF is what goes out with your applications.

Freshers, do not put this off because you feel you have little to show. Projects, internships and coursework fill a first resume honestly, and a plain, clean one is all the app needs. You can replace it later, and later applications use the new one.

Sign in to your first job site

Profile on Job Sites is the menu in the sidebar where your job site accounts live. Open it, choose a site, and the app opens its own browser at that site's sign-in page. You type the login yourself. The app never sees or stores your password, and the sign-in stays on your computer.

One site is enough to get going, so pick the one with the most jobs for you. In India that is probably Naukri, with Internshala if you are looking for internships. In the United States, start with LinkedIn or Indeed. Once you have signed in, the app notices and shows the site as available to pick for a run.

Two useful things happen on this screen. First, the app can fill in your profile on the other sites from the one you keep in the app: it adds to those profiles, never removes from them, and a fill in progress has a Stop button beside it. Second, this is where you come back whenever a sign-in expires.

If you connect LinkedIn, it is worth knowing the app's posture: it works on Easy Apply postings from your own logged-in session, at a steady human pace, and it stops the moment a site pushes back. Some sites restrict automated use in their terms, so read the Terms of Use on this site before you connect anything. The full walkthrough, including what to do when a sign-in will not go through, is in signing in to your job sites.

Choose your job titles and locations

The app searches for jobs using your preferences: job titles, locations, on-site, hybrid or remote, seniority, a minimum salary if you set one, and any companies to exclude. Titles and locations shape everything, so take a minute over them rather than going with the first thing that comes to mind.

For titles, use the words employers actually advertise with, not the one in your head. If you are a fresher, include the entry-level variants of the role you want. A first data job might look like this:

Data Analyst, Business Analyst, Reporting Analyst, MIS Analyst

Experienced people should list variants too, because the same work is advertised as Backend Developer, Backend Engineer and Platform Engineer depending on the site. A handful of close variants makes a good list; a pile of unrelated titles brings back jobs you would never read.

For locations, list the cities you would genuinely work in. In India that might be Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Pune; in the United States, the metro areas within reach. Remote is its own preference, separate from the location list: if you only want remote work, say so there and keep the locations broad.

These preferences are yours to change at any time, and each run uses whatever is in them when it starts. If a run brings back jobs that do not fit, tighten a title or drop a location and start again. That loop, search, adjust, run, is the main control you have over the quality of what the app applies to.

Start applying

Everything so far feeds one button. On the Dashboard, put in your job requirements, select the job sites to apply on, and press Start applying. On the site list, the sites you are signed into come first; the rest are greyed out until you sign in, with a hint pointing you to Profile on Job Sites. What happens after you press the button is worth understanding once.

What the app checks first

Before it opens anything, the app checks that a run can succeed. If a required detail is missing from your profile, it stops and names the exact fields. If your resume is missing, it says so. If you forgot to sign in to a site you picked, it tells you and offers to open the sign-in page, but does not refuse to start. All of this happens before a single posting is opened, which is far better than discovering it halfway through a form.

What a run looks like

While the run works, the Apply through job sites card wears a green outline with a light travelling along it, and the applications dial lights up green. A Now running card shows the job title, the company, and the phase the app is on, with a Watch live button beside it.

Press Watch live and the built-in browser comes forward. You will see the app open the posting, fill each field, and step through the form, with a strip above the page naming the application it is filling and a Stop watching button for when you have seen enough. Or press nothing: the app keeps working with the browser tucked away, and a small dot on the Browser item in the sidebar tells you it is still going.

The app applies to one job at a time and waits between applications. That wait is pacing, and it exists to protect your accounts, so leave room for it: a run is a steady walk, not a sprint. You set the gap in Settings, and the careful end of the range is the right place to start. The search bar at the top of the app doubles as a command menu, so you can start or pause a run from it, and it searches your queued jobs and saved answers as you type. The whole anatomy of a run, from queue to submitted, is covered in apply through job sites.

When a question comes back to you

Sooner or later a form asks something your profile cannot answer: a question about one employer's process, or a tool only you know you have used. The app does not guess. It stops that application and raises a Boost card: a screenshot of just that question, the job title and company, the question as plain text, and three choices. Skip the job, let AI answer, or answer manually.

If you let AI answer, it draws on your profile and the job description and gives the answer you would most likely give, then saves it like any other answer. If you answer manually, the app hands the page back with everything else already filled in and the one empty question highlighted on screen. Type your answer, press the button that says you are done, and the app takes over again. It then asks whether this answer will be the same for other jobs. If yes, it saves the answer for reuse and never asks you that question again. If it was specific to this job, it stores it for this job only.

This is why the first applications are the slowest and the tenth is quicker. Every question the app has to ask you gets remembered, so the cards thin out as your answers library grows.

Two kinds of questions always come to you, whatever else the app answers by itself. Consent questions, such as whether you consent to a background check, are decisions, not profile fields, and the app leaves them to you. And it never fills in passwords, government ID numbers or bank details; if a form asks for those, it stops and shows you the question instead.

What to expect in the first days

The first days have a rhythm: runs go out, a few things come back to you, and your answers library fills up. Here is what to keep an eye on, and what you can ignore.

The applications board

The Dashboard keeps a board of applications, one row per job, newest first, each with the logo of the site it went through. A green outline means sent. A yellow outline means the app stopped on something and is waiting on you. Beside the board, the totals show applications sent and cold mails sent, each with a this-week figure next to it. Those are counts, not quotas.

Jobs the app skipped are not listed on the board; it shows progress, not everything it touched. The queue of jobs still to come sits above it, and you can remove a job from the queue if something about it puts you off.

Replies do not arrive in the app. Recruiters reach you the normal way, at the email address and phone number on your profile, so check that inbox regularly. An interview invitation sitting unread costs you exactly what it would have before you had an app filling forms for you.

Answers worth reviewing

Answers, in the sidebar, is the library of everything the app has answered. It is searchable and sortable, and every entry shows the question, the answer, where it came from (your profile, you, or AI) and how many times it has been used.

The app submits applications without showing you each answer for approval first, which is exactly why this library exists: it is how you see what went out, and how you correct what will go out. Skim it once after your first run, and use the filter for AI-answered answers when you do, because those are the ones the app worked out on its own. If you would phrase something differently, edit the answer once and every future application uses your version. If an answer is simply wrong, delete it, and the app will ask you again instead of quietly reusing it.

Checks that need you

A few things always stop for a human, and they announce themselves as a banner across the Dashboard, above everything else.

  • A security check, such as the puzzles sites use to confirm a human is present. The app never solves these. It brings the browser forward and waits for you to solve it, then the run continues.
  • A sign-in that expired. The banner asks you to sign in again, in the same built-in browser, and the run resumes once you have.
  • A site asking the app to slow down. The app pauses everything rather than pushing into a site that has pushed back.
  • An employer account. Some companies make you create an account on their own career page before it will take an application. The app parks that posting and hands you the link to do it yourself.

One habit worth keeping for life, with or without the app: a form that asks for bank details, a payment, or a government ID in order to apply is not a real job. The app will not fill those fields; it stops and shows them to you. Treat anything like that the same way yourself.

And the honest footnote: some sites restrict automated use in their terms. The app works from your own sign-in, paces itself, and stops at the first pushback. Read the Terms of Use on this site so you know where you stand before you connect your accounts.

Every case where a job needs you, from Boost cards to expired sign-ins to employer accounts, is gathered in when a job needs you.

Your first day checklist

Everything above, in the order you will actually do it:

  1. Install GMAFJ from the Microsoft Store and create your free account.
  2. Fill in your profile, or upload your resume and fill the gaps it leaves.
  3. Get right the details every form asks for: your phone number with its country code, the countries you can work in, sponsorship, and notice period and salary where they apply to you.
  4. Save a resume PDF, generated from your profile or your own upload, and look at the preview.
  5. Sign in to one job site under Profile on Job Sites.
  6. Add your job titles and locations, and set on-site, hybrid or remote.
  7. On the Dashboard, put in your job requirements, select the site you signed into, and press Start applying.
  8. Press Watch live for the first application or two, so you can see how a form gets filled.
  9. Answer any question that comes back as a card, and tell the app whether to reuse each answer.
  10. Open Answers, filter for AI-answered, and fix anything you would phrase differently.
  11. Set a pace in Settings that fits your day, and let the run continue.

That is the whole setup. From the second day on, the app needs you less: every answer it saves is one it never asks again, and the applications keep going out while you get on with your day.
